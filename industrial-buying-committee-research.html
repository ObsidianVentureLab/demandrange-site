How to research roles in an industrial buying committee

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 25 November 2026

There is no official public list of who decides an industrial purchase. "Buying committee" on this page is a research label for the functions that can shape a plant purchase at a named account, starting with maintenance, engineering, procurement and environment, health and safety (EHS). It is not a published body with a fixed membership, and it is not a list of people to contact. This page shows how to research those functions account by account in Ireland, the UK and the EU, and where that reading goes wrong.

Demand Range researches industrial accounts in Ireland, the UK and the EU. This page owns the method for mapping roles inside an account. Account fit is covered on industrial ideal customer profile. The wider method is explained in what industrial account research is and in our methodology. Scoring and timing are covered on industrial account prioritisation.

01 / key facts

Key facts

Companies House; Companies Registration Office; ILO ISCO-08; Office for National Statistics SOC 2020; Health and Safety Authority; Health and Safety Executive; checked 24 November 2026.

  • Official company registers record legal officers and company particulars, not a buying committee. On GOV.UK, free company details include company information (for example the registered address) and current and resigned officers. The Companies Registration Office (CRO) is the central repository of public statutory information on Irish companies and business names. Director and secretary details on the CRO register must be kept up to date, with changes filed within 14 days on Form B10. Most company types must have one secretary and a minimum of two directors. None of these records is a list of who buys.[1][2][3]
  • Official occupational classifications describe kinds of jobs, not who buys at a named plant. The International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO-08) defines a job as "a set of tasks and duties performed, or meant to be performed, by one person, including for an employer or in self employment." An occupation is a "set of jobs whose main tasks and duties are characterized by a high degree of similarity." The UK's official Standard Occupational Classification 2020 (SOC 2020) classifies jobs by skill level and skill content, and official mapping to ISCO-08 is published with it. Use these classifications as labels when you record a function.[4][5]
  • Health and safety law assigns duties to the employer, not to a purchase signatory. In Ireland, sections 19 and 20 of the Safety, Health and Welfare at Work Act 2005 require a risk assessment and a written Safety Statement. Official Health and Safety Authority guidance says a Safety Statement should contain an organisation chart of the safety and health management structure and, as a minimum, the senior post with delegated responsibility for safety and health. In Great Britain, the Health and Safety Executive describes the Health and Safety at Work Act as the main law governing health and safety at work, and says the Act places general duties on employers. A legal EHS duty is not evidence that that post signs a purchase order.[6][7]
  • Named contacts and outreach are separate scopes, not included by default. Record functions, sources and dates. Do not treat this method as a way to build a contact list.
02 / what a buying committee is and what it is not

What a buying committee is, and what it is not

A buying committee, as used here, is the set of functions at an account that can specify, influence, approve or process a purchase of plant, equipment, spares or related services. The starting set on this page is maintenance, engineering, procurement and EHS. Other posts, such as site or operations leadership, belong in the record when a source shows they sponsor the work.

It is not an official term used by company registers or by ISCO-08 or SOC 2020. It is not a fixed number of people, and it is not the same thing as the directors and secretaries on the public register.[1][2][3][4][5]

It is also not a licence to collect personal data or to make contact. A public page, a register extract or a Safety Statement can justify a research question about a function. It does not, on its own, show that a budget has been approved, that a tender will run, or that anyone at the company is open to a supplier approach.

The industrial ideal customer profile page asks whether an account fits. This page asks which functions inside an account you still need to understand before you can brief a written research request. How accounts are scored, including timing, is a separate step on industrial account prioritisation.

03 / the functions to research

The functions to research

Record each function from dated public sources. Leave a row as "not stated" when no source supports it. The labels below are yours, for research. They are not official categories, even where they overlap with kinds of jobs described in ISCO-08 and SOC 2020.[4][5]

Function What to record from sources Do not infer
Maintenance and reliability A maintenance, reliability or technician function tied to the site, and any wording about uptime, spares or like-for-like replacement That a spare or service contract is about to be placed
Engineering and projects An engineering, projects or technical function, and any wording about a line, a specification or a capital project That a specification has been approved or that a tender will run
Procurement and commercial A buying, procurement or supplier-quality function, and any published supplier process That this post will run a particular purchase, or that group procurement serves a given site
Environment, health and safety (EHS) An EHS or occupational safety function, or a senior post with delegated safety responsibility, as a public Safety Statement or similar source states it That the post signs the purchase order
Senior officers and site leadership Officers as the register lists them, and any public statement that a director or site leader sponsors the work That a listed officer is the buyer

A small plant may combine several of these functions in one post. A large group may split site engineering from group procurement. Those are research cautions, not official statistics. Do not fill a missing function by assuming a "typical" committee.

04 / how to research them account by account

How to research them account by account

You are compiling a research record, not harvesting names and not preparing outreach.

  1. Confirm the legal entity and its officers on the official register. In the UK, start with the GOV.UK service for getting information about a company, which includes current and resigned officers among the free details. In Ireland, start with the CRO register for the company's public statutory record, and treat director and secretary filings as evidence of legal officers, not of buyers. Record the registered name, the officer posts the source actually lists, and the date you checked.[1][2][3]
  2. Read the company's own public pages. About, operations, careers, and supplier or procurement pages are often the only public description of how the firm buys or who specifies equipment. Record the function, any site the page names, the URL and the date checked. If the company has more than one site, do not assign a group function to the plant you already research unless the page names that site.
  3. Read public job advertisements as evidence of a function. An advertised maintenance, engineering, procurement or EHS post can support a function at a named site. It is not evidence of a live purchase. Record only what the advertisement states.
  4. Use health and safety documents for the EHS function, not as a contact list. Where a Safety Statement or similar official document is lawfully available, use it to see how safety duties are assigned, including any senior post with delegated responsibility for safety and health. Official Irish guidance also describes a "competent person" by training, experience and knowledge, and says an employer should use a competent employee on safety matters when one is available, or obtain advice from outside the organisation if not. Record the function and the duty. Do not copy names into a calling list.[6][7]
  5. Write the evidence note, then stop. Capture the function, the site if stated, the source and the date checked. Leave gaps as "not stated". Decide the next research action. Do not move from the record to outreach.

Fictional examples. The companies, sites and pages below are invented, to show how the method works. None is a real account, and none should be treated as evidence about any real employer.

  • Fictional company B-A, Ireland. The company's careers page advertises a "site engineering manager — new packing line" and a "maintenance technician", both naming one manufacturing site. A supplier page describes a vendor-approval process. Record engineering, maintenance and procurement as functions supported by those pages. The CRO record lists two directors. Do not treat those directors as the buyers unless a source says they sponsor the work.
  • Fictional company B-B, the UK. Companies House shows current officers and no other public clue to how the firm buys. There is no engineering, maintenance or procurement page. Leave those functions as "not stated". Do not invent a committee from the officer list.
  • Fictional company B-C. A group page titled "head of procurement — Europe" names the group but no site. This is not a site-level function. Do not assign it to an Irish or UK plant until a source names the site.

These examples only illustrate the method.

05 / where this reading goes wrong

Where this reading goes wrong

These are the usual ways a buying-committee record gets over-read:

  • Officers on the register are not the committee. Directors and secretaries are legal officers. They may sponsor a purchase, and often they do not run the specification or the order. Treat a register extract as an officer list, then look for a second source before you record a buying function against a named officer.[1][2][3]
  • A job title is not sign-off. "Buyer", "project engineer" or "EHS manager" on a page or an advertisement tells you a function exists, or is being advertised. It does not tell you that this post will approve a particular buy.
  • A group role is not a site role. Group procurement or a central engineering team may sit in another country, or may not serve the plant you care about. A page that names the group, but not the site, is not a site-level record.
  • Pages and charts go stale. Careers pages, organisation charts and supplier guides can outlive the structure they describe. Keep the date you checked with the record, and prefer a second, later source when the first is old.
  • Not every plant has four separate posts. If you cannot find a distinct procurement or EHS function, leave it blank. Do not complete the set from a template.
  • A Safety Statement is not a contact list. Official Irish guidance expects a safety organisation chart and a senior post with delegated responsibility for safety and health. That supports an EHS function in your record. It does not authorise outreach to the people named on the chart.[6]
  • There is no official "typical" committee size. This page does not quote survey figures on how many people sit on an industrial buying committee, or on how often each function leads a buy, because none of the official sources cited here publishes those statistics.
06 / how this sits with other account research

How this sits with other account research

Mapping functions inside an account is most useful after you already know the account is worth a closer look. It helps you see which questions are still open: who specifies, who signs, who can block a change on safety grounds, and whether buying is done at the site or in a group team. It should not, on its own, be treated as a live opportunity or as a reason to make contact.

Useful companions, each with its own guide, include:

  • Industrial ideal customer profile. Whether the account fits, before you invest time in roles inside it.
  • Industrial account prioritisation. How fit, timing and other signals are scored. A clearer map of functions can improve a briefing; it is not itself a timing signal.
  • Methodology. How Demand Range builds account research across Ireland, the UK and the EU.
  • Sample account research. The kind of written output this work supports.

A practical sequence:

  1. Confirm the legal entity and the site.
  2. Record each buying function the public sources will support, with URLs and dates.
  3. Leave gaps as open questions, not as guesses.
  4. Use the record to brief the next research step, or to decide that the account is not yet clear enough to go further.
  5. Do not move from the function map to outreach.

07 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

Who decides on industrial purchases?
There is no official public list of who decides an industrial purchase. Research the functions that can shape the buy at that account, starting with maintenance, engineering, procurement and EHS, and record only what each public source states. Company officers, a job title or a safety chart are not proof that a named person will sign.[1][2][6]
Are company directors the buyers?
No. Official registers record directors and secretaries as legal officers, together with the company's registered name and address. In Ireland, officer details must be updated within 14 days of a change. In the UK, current and resigned officers are listed by Companies House. An officer may sponsor a purchase, but the register is not a buying-committee list.[1][2][3]
Which roles should I research first?
Start with four functions: maintenance, engineering, procurement and EHS. These are research labels, not an official or complete set. A small plant may combine them in one post, and a large group may split site and group buying. Add operations leadership when a source shows that post sponsors the work. Leave a function blank when no source supports it.
Where can I research these roles for a named account?
Start with the official company register to confirm the legal entity and its officers. Then read the company's own public pages, including any supplier or careers pages, and any lawfully obtained Safety Statement that sets out safety duties. Record the function, the source and the date checked. Do not build a contact list or start outreach from these sources.[1][2][3][6]
Can I use this research to contact people at the company?
Not as part of this method. This page maps functions inside an account so you know what to check next, not names to message. Named contacts and outreach are separate scopes, not included by default, and they need their own legal basis. A sample of account research shows the kind of written output this supports.

"You can get some details about a company for free, including:" company information, for example registered address and date of incorporation; current and resigned officers; document images; mortgage charge data; previous company names; and insolvency information.   2. Companies Registration Office, Access to Data. "The CRO is the central repository of public statutory information on Irish companies and business names." The page lists CORE entity fields including the registered entity number and name, registration date and status, last and next annual return dates, and related address fields, plus submission metadata. That published field list does not include a buying function. Checked 24 November 2026.
  3. Companies Registration Office, Company Officers – Directors and Secretaries. "Director and Secretary details on the Register must be kept up-to-date." The page states that the CRO register should be updated with any changes to a company's directors and secretaries within 14 days of the change, through Form B10 via CORE, and that "Most Company types are required to have one secretary and a minimum of two directors." The page's discussion of single-director exceptions is not used on this page. Checked 24 November 2026.
  4. ILOSTAT, International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO). ISCO-08 defines a job as "a set of tasks and duties performed, or meant to be performed, by one person, including for an employer or in self employment," and an occupation as a "set of jobs whose main tasks and duties are characterized by a high degree of similarity." The page sets out the ISCO-08 hierarchical structure (major, sub-major, minor and unit groups). Checked 24 November 2026.
  5. Office for National Statistics, SOC 2020. "The current Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) for the UK is published in three volumes." "Within the context of the classification, jobs are classified in terms of their skill level and skill content." The page also points to official SOC 2020 to ISCO-08 mapping information. Checked 24 November 2026.
  6. Health and Safety Authority, Safety Statement and Risk Assessment. Section 19 of the Safety, Health and Welfare at Work Act 2005 requires employers, and those who control workplaces to any extent, to identify hazards and assess risks. Section 20 requires a written Safety Statement. Official guidance on that page states: "The Safety Statement should contain an organisation chart showing the safety and health management structure and the names and responsibilities of key personnel. As a minimum, it must include the name of the person at senior management or director level with delegated responsibility for safety and health in the company." The same page defines a competent person by training, experience and knowledge, and summarises section 18 on using a competent employee or obtaining advice from outside the organisation. This page uses that guidance to identify an EHS function, not to collect names. The Authority page also displays contact telephone numbers; those numbers are not repeated here. Checked 24 November 2026.
  7. Health and Safety Executive, Introduction to managing health and safety, section 4, The law. "This is the main law governing health and safety at work in Great Britain." "The Act places general duties on employers, and some self-employed people to do what is 'reasonably practicable' to ensure your health and safety and that of others who may be affected by what you do or do not do." The page also states that under the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations, "employers must put arrangements in place to control health and safety risks." Checked 24 November 2026.
