EU ETS installations as industrial account signals

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 10 November 2026

A plant listed in the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) is a combustion or process installation that must report verified emissions and surrender allowances. The Commission's Union Registry files name the installation, its permit, its main activity and its verified emissions by year. A listing is an energy and carbon signal at a known site. It is not a buyer, a budget or a project.

This guide is for suppliers of burners, boilers, heat pumps, heat recovery, compressed air, process heating, metering and carbon-capture equipment. It explains which official files to download, what a row can support, and how the UK ETS covers Great Britain and most of Northern Ireland alongside the EU system.

01 / key facts

Key facts

Checked 10 November 2026.

  • The Commission says the EU ETS applies in all EU Member States, in Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway, and in Northern Ireland for electricity generation. It covers emissions from "around 10,000 installations in the energy sector and manufacturing industry".[5]
  • The Commission's Union Registry page lists "Verified Emissions for 2025" (posted 9 April 2026) and "Compliance data for 2025" (posted 5 October 2026).[1] The verified-emissions file states an extraction date of 1 April 2026.[2]
  • By our count of the 2025 compliance file, 8,533 stationary installations had a compliance status for 2025, of which 8,367 were in the EU-27 and 118 in Ireland.[3]
  • The Commission reported a 1.3% fall in EU ETS emissions in 2025 compared with 2024, and a 2.5% fall for energy-intensive industries.[6]
  • The UK ETS recorded 71,124,762 tCO2e from installations in 2025. 717 operators held an open installation account, and 683 had a surrender obligation for 2025.[12]
02 / what an ets listing tells a supplier

What an ETS listing tells a supplier

An installation is in the EU ETS because it carries out an activity listed in the ETS Directive above a threshold. The activity codes in the Commission's files include "Combustion installations with a rated thermal input exceeding 20 MW", cement clinker, lime, glass, ceramics, pulp, paper, steel, refining, bulk chemicals and hydrogen.[2][3]

For account research, that gives you three facts you can cite:

  • The site burns fuel or runs a process at scale. A permit and an activity code are public evidence that the plant has significant combustion or process emissions.
  • The site has a carbon cost and a reporting duty. Each operator must surrender allowances to cover its verified emissions, and the Registry records the annual reconciliation.[1]
  • The trend is visible. Verified emissions by year show whether a site's emissions are rising, falling or have stopped.[2]

It does not tell you who decides on equipment, whether a project is funded, which supplier is in place or what the plant plans to buy. A falling emissions line can mean an efficiency project, lower output or a closure. Treat the row as a reason to research the site, not as a lead.

03 / route 1 the eu union registry files

Route 1: the EU Union Registry files

The Union Registry is the Commission's online database of allowances, accounts and compliance. The Commission says data on free allocation, verified emissions, compliance status and account holders is publicly available on the Union Registry website.[1][7] For research at scale, the Excel files on the Commission's Union Registry page are easier to filter.[1]

Verified emissions file. One row per account, with registry country code, installation name, installation identifier, permit identifier, main activity code, free allocation and verified emissions for each year since 2008.[2] The file's notes say data from 2013 is not directly comparable with 2012 and before, because the scope widened in phase III.[2] It also holds aircraft and maritime operator accounts, so filter those out for plant research.

Compliance file. One row per account, with the compliance code, the latest compliance year, total verified emissions and surrendered allowances, the first and last emissions years, and account closure.[3] By our count, its verified-emissions and surrender columns are cumulative from 2021, not annual, so use the verified emissions file for single years.

List of operators. The Commission's list of operators in the EU ETS (dated April 2024 on the Registry page) adds the account holder name, the company registration number, the LEI where given, the permit, the activity type and a contact address.[1][4] The registration number and LEI are what let you match an installation to a legal entity in a company register.

04 / ireland the epa permit list

Ireland: the EPA permit list

In Ireland the EPA issues greenhouse gas emissions permits under the European Communities (Greenhouse Gas Emissions Trading) Regulations 2024 (S.I. No. 470 of 2024).[8] Its "Access to current permits" page lists the operator name, the installation name and the permit registration number for each permitted stationary installation.[9]

By our count on 10 November 2026, that page listed 122 permitted installations, with 122 distinct registration numbers, under 89 distinct operator names as written.[9] The Commission's 2025 compliance file has 118 Irish stationary installations with a 2025 compliance status, by our count.[3] The two sources are not the same list on the same date: the EPA page shows permits in force now, while the Commission file shows installations with a compliance status for the 2025 scheme year. Record which one you used.

By our count of the compliance file, 107 of the 118 Irish installations are coded as combustion of fuels. The rest are cement clinker (4), lime (3), and one each for refining, ceramics, gypsum or plasterboard, and greenhouse gas capture.[3] The EPA list includes food, dairy, pharmaceutical and data-centre operators as well as power generators, which is why the operator name matters more than the code.[9]

For Irish site evidence beyond ETS, such as EPA licences and PRTR returns, see Irish environmental register evidence.

05 / route 2 the uk ets for great britain and northern ireland

Route 2: the UK ETS for Great Britain and Northern Ireland

Since 1 January 2021, installations in Great Britain, and installations in Northern Ireland other than electricity generators, have been in the UK ETS, not the EU ETS. The UK ETS Authority is the UK Government, the Scottish Government, the Welsh Government and DAERA for Northern Ireland.[12] The exception is electricity generation in Northern Ireland: the UK ETS Authority says six power generating stations there stay in the EU ETS, to maintain a single ETS price for Irish electricity generators and the Single Electricity Market on the island of Ireland.[12] The Commission also lists Northern Ireland for electricity generation in the EU ETS scope.[5]

GOV.UK publishes UK ETS scheme reports in one collection, which links to the UK ETS Registry reporting page.[10][13] The current official compliance publication is the Compliance Report - emissions and surrenders on that registry page. It covers operator and aircraft operator accounts for the 2021 to 2025 scheme years, and the registry page shows it as published on 22 June 2026 (its "Published on" date).[13][14] The DESNZ report Functioning of the UK carbon market, 2025 (published 4 November 2026) summarises the same scheme year.[11][12]

Key UK figures from that report:[12]

  • Installation thresholds include combustion units with a total rated thermal input above 20MW. Hospitals and small emitters (below 25,000 tCO2e a year, subject to conditions) can have emissions targets instead of surrender obligations, and ultra-small emitters below 2,500 tCO2e a year are not required to hold a permit.
  • Total recorded UK ETS emissions were 79,716,174 tCO2e in 2025, compared with 85,610,435 tCO2e in 2024, a fall of 6.9%.
  • Industrial emissions fell from 35,472,613 to 29,998,594 tCO2e (−15.4%). Power emissions rose from 29,102,742 to 29,560,211 tCO2e (+1.6%).
  • 717 operators held an open installation account. The report notes that an open account does not necessarily mean the installation is open.

The UK compliance report is more useful than the EU files for sector work, because each row carries a NACE code and description as well as the regulator and the installation name.[14] By our count, 669 open installation accounts had 2025 recorded emissions above zero. Their 2025 figures sum to 71,124,762 tCO2e, the same total as the DESNZ report.[12][14]

06 / a method from registry row to account record

A method: from registry row to account record

  1. Download the files, and record the dates. Use the Commission's verified emissions and compliance files for the EU, and the UK ETS compliance report for Great Britain and Northern Ireland.[1][13] Note the extraction or publication date on each.
  2. Remove aviation and maritime accounts. In the EU files, drop activity code 10 (aircraft operators) and 50 (maritime operators).[2] In the UK file, keep operator holding accounts only.[14]
  3. Filter by activity and country. Pick the activity codes your equipment serves, such as combustion of fuels for burners and heat recovery, or glass, ceramics and lime for kilns and furnaces.
  4. Read the emissions trend. Compare at least three years of verified emissions. A step change is a question for research, not an answer.
  5. Match the installation to a company. Use the operator list's registration number or LEI, then the national company register. Add the site's environmental permits where they exist; our guide to the EU industrial emissions portal shows the E-PRTR and IED side.
  6. Add the energy context. Large energy users may also have energy-audit duties. Our energy audit signals guide explains how to read those alongside ETS data.
  7. Record what the source supports. Write down the row, the year, the file and the check date. Keep inferences, such as "likely to need heat recovery", in a separate field. Our opportunity intelligence work follows this pattern.
07 / a worked example wholly fictional

A worked example (wholly fictional)

The supplier, operator, installation and all figures below are invented. No real installation, company, permit or purchase is represented.

An invented heat-recovery supplier filters the EU verified emissions file for combustion of fuels in two EU countries. It finds "Fictional Dairy Operator D", whose installation's verified emissions fell for two years and then rose again.

  • Registry row: combustion of fuels; verified emissions for five years; permit identifier recorded.
  • Operator list: company registration number matched to a national register entry with the same legal name.
  • Account record: steam and hot-water demand is plausible. The reason for the rise is unknown. No project, budget or supplier is known.
  • Next check: the next verified emissions file, plus the site's environmental permit for any new boiler or combined heat and power unit.

The supplier adds the site to its research list. It does not treat the emissions rise as a purchase signal on its own.

08 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

Where can I download a list of EU ETS installations?
From the European Commission's Union Registry page, which posts yearly verified emissions files and compliance files as Excel downloads.[1] The 2025 compliance file was posted on 5 October 2026.[1] Each row gives the registry country, installation name and identifier, permit and main activity.[2][3] Filter out aircraft and maritime accounts before counting plants.
How many installations are in the EU ETS?
The Commission says the EU ETS covers around 10,000 installations in the energy sector and manufacturing industry.[5] By our count, the 2025 compliance file lists 8,533 stationary installations with a 2025 compliance status, 8,367 of them in the EU-27.[3] Counts change each year as installations open, close or fall out of scope, so date your figure.
Does an EU ETS listing mean a plant will buy decarbonisation equipment?
No. A listing confirms that the installation carries out an activity in scope, reports verified emissions and must surrender allowances.[1][2] It shows a carbon cost and an emissions trend at a known site. It does not show a budget, a project, a decision-maker or a supplier. Treat it as a research signal and confirm the rest from public sources.
Are UK plants still in the EU ETS?
Mostly not. Since 2021, installations in Great Britain, and in Northern Ireland other than electricity generators, report under the UK ETS, with compliance data on the UK ETS Registry.[12][13] The exception is six power generating stations in Northern Ireland, which stay in the EU ETS to keep a single carbon price for electricity generators on the island of Ireland.[12]
Why are most EU ETS installations coded as combustion of fuels?
Because that activity covers any site burning fuel in combustion units above the threshold, not only power stations.[2][3] By our count it is 60.8% of installations with a 2025 compliance status.[3] Many are boilers or combined heat and power plants at food, pharmaceutical or chemical sites, so check the operator and register before assigning a sector.
