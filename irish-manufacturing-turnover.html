How is Irish manufacturing turnover moving?

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 25 November 2026

CSO's monthly manufacturing turnover index shows a sharp rise in 2025 and a fall in 2026 so far, with large month-to-month swings. By our count, from CSO's unadjusted monthly cells (2021 = 100), the 12-month average rose from 114.7 in 2024 to 133.0 in 2025, a 15.9% increase. The January–September 2026 average was 112.8, 18.2% lower than the same period in 2025. CSO's own seasonally adjusted reading for July to September 2026, which is provisional, was 0.4% higher than April to June 2026 and 10.2% lower than the same three months in 2025.

Demand Range researches industrial accounts in Ireland, the UK and the EU. This page owns one thing: CSO monthly turnover for Irish manufacturing (NACE 10–33). It is a value index of sales, not a production-volume measure, and it includes manufacturing in Ireland and manufacturing abroad. For turnover growth by EU country, see EU manufacturing turnover by country. Eurostat does not publish an all-manufacturing turnover figure for Ireland in those tables.

01 / key facts

Key facts

CSO table MIM05 and CSO Industrial Production and Turnover, September 2026, published 9 November 2026; checked 24 November 2026.

  • By our count, the unadjusted manufacturing turnover index, 2021 = 100, averaged 133.0 in 2025, up 15.9% on the 2024 average of 114.7.[1]
  • By our count, the January–September 2026 average was 112.8, 18.2% lower than the January–September 2025 average of 137.9.[1]
  • From July to September 2026, quarterly turnover in manufacturing was 0.4% higher than in April to June 2026 and 10.2% lower than in July to September 2025, according to CSO's seasonally adjusted figures. Those latest three-month figures are provisional.[2]
  • In September 2026 the seasonally adjusted turnover index was 104.6, down 15.7% on August 2026 and 33.1% lower than in September 2025. The unadjusted September 2026 index was 119.0, 34.0% lower than a year earlier. September 2026 figures are provisional.[2]
  • CSO recommends a longer-term view, because monthly volatility can be very high. The results include manufacturing in Ireland and manufacturing abroad, either by foreign subsidiaries or by foreign subcontractors.[2][3]
  • Several published sub-sectors were still higher in January–September 2026 than a year earlier, by our count, including bakery and farinaceous products (+23.0%), transport equipment (+20.7%) and wood products (+14.6%). CSO does not publish turnover for chemicals, pharmaceuticals, computers and electronics, or machinery.[1][2]

03 / the 2025 rise

The 2025 rise

By our count, from the 12 official unadjusted monthly cells, manufacturing turnover averaged 133.0 in 2025, 15.9% higher than the 2024 average of 114.7. The rise was not even through the year. Unadjusted turnover was much higher than a year earlier in several months, including March 2025 (190.0, against 99.4 in March 2024) and September 2025 (180.3, against 130.5 in September 2024), and lower in June, July and October.[1]

Electrical equipment had the largest published 2025 rise among the sub-sectors with complete turnover cells: its average index was 254.6, up 56.2% on 2024, by our count. Wood products rose 18.4%, meat 12.7%, rubber and plastic products 9.8%, and dairy 9.5%. Transport equipment fell 5.7%, and other food products 8.8%. Food products as a whole (NACE 10) were almost unchanged, up 0.5%.[1]

This is a current-price sales index, so a rise can reflect prices as well as volumes. For volume, use a production index, which is a different series.

04 / the 2026 figures so far

The 2026 figures so far

By our count, the January–September 2026 unadjusted average was 112.8, 18.2% lower than the January–September 2025 average of 137.9. That nine-month comparison hides months that were higher than a year earlier: unadjusted turnover was up 6.5% in June 2026 and 13.6% in July 2026, then 34.0% lower in September.[1][2]

CSO recommends that analysts look at a longer period than a single month, and its own commentary uses rolling three-month figures. On that basis, seasonally adjusted turnover in July to September 2026 was 0.4% higher than in April to June 2026 and 10.2% lower than in July to September 2025. Those latest three-month figures, and the September 2026 month itself, are provisional.[2][3]

The seasonally adjusted September 2026 index was 104.6, 15.7% lower than August 2026 and 33.1% lower than September 2025. A single month that sharp is why CSO asks readers not to lean on the latest month alone when judging the direction of sales.[2]

There are no published turnover figures for chemicals (20), pharmaceuticals (21), computer, electronic and optical products (26), machinery (28), or for CSO's Modern and Traditional sector groupings. Those gaps are official. This page does not fill them from production indices or from any other table.[1][2]

06 / what the swings mean for sales timing

What the swings mean for sales timing

CSO says the scale of contract manufacturing and outsourcing in Irish industry has increased since 2015, and that very high monthly volatility may be present in these indices. It recommends a longer-term view, and its own commentary uses rolling three-month periods. The figures cover manufacturing undertaken on behalf of industrial enterprises with headquarters in Ireland, including manufacturing abroad by foreign subsidiaries or foreign subcontractors.[2][3]

For a supplier, that has three practical limits. First, a single month is a poor trigger for outreach timing: September 2026's seasonally adjusted fall of 15.7% sits in a quarter that CSO recorded as 0.4% higher than the previous quarter. Second, the national manufacturing total can fall while some published sub-sectors are still rising, as in January–September 2026. Third, because the index includes activity abroad, a rise or fall is not, on its own, evidence that plants in Ireland are busier or quieter.

The survey covers the larger industrial local units that account for the bulk of output, in general those with 20 or more persons engaged in the 2021 base year. It is a national sales index. It does not name plants, buyers or budgets, and it does not convert to a euro value of sales.[3]

07 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

What does the Irish manufacturing turnover index measure?
It tracks the change in sales of industrial products, whether made in the same month or earlier. It is a CSO value index, 2021 = 100, reflecting both sales volume and the price level. This page uses manufacturing (NACE 10–33). The figures include manufacturing in Ireland and abroad, and they are not a production-volume measure.[1][2][3]
Did Irish manufacturing turnover rise in 2025?
Yes. By our count, from CSO's unadjusted monthly manufacturing turnover cells, the 12-month average rose from 114.7 in 2024 to 133.0 in 2025, a 15.9% increase. The rise was uneven across months and sectors. Electrical equipment had the largest published increase, 56.2%, by our count. Transport equipment fell 5.7%.[1]
Is Irish manufacturing turnover falling in 2026?
The nine-month average is lower than a year earlier, but the latest quarter is not a straight fall. By our count, January–September 2026 was 18.2% below January–September 2025. CSO's provisional, seasonally adjusted figure for July to September 2026 was 0.4% higher than the previous quarter and 10.2% lower than a year earlier.[1][2]
Why do the monthly figures swing so much?
CSO says monthly volatility can be very high, in part because the indices include contract manufacturing and outsourcing, and because they include manufacturing abroad as well as in Ireland. It recommends a longer-term view and uses rolling three-month periods in its own commentary. Read any single month, including a sharp September, in that light.[2][3]
Which manufacturing sectors grew in 2026?
Among sectors with published turnover, the largest January–September 2026 rises, by our count, were bakery and farinaceous products (+23.0%), transport equipment (+20.7%) and wood products (+14.6%). Dairy fell 10.9%, and electrical equipment was almost unchanged, at −0.7%. CSO does not publish turnover for chemicals, pharmaceuticals, computers and electronics, or machinery.[1][2]
Can a turnover index tell a supplier who will buy?
No. It shows whether sales in a sector or in manufacturing as a whole are rising or falling, in value terms. It does not name plants, buyers or budgets, and a change can include activity abroad. Use it to see which published sectors are moving, then turn to company-level account research to build a list.[1][2]
