Manufacturing greenhouse gas emissions by EU country

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 24 November 2026

Manufacturing emitted 688.4 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent across the EU-27 in 2024, about 26.2% of the greenhouse gases from all production activities, by our count. In Ireland the share was 11.1% (6.72 million tonnes, an estimate), the third lowest in the EU, by our count. The figures come from Eurostat's air emissions accounts by NACE Rev. 2 activity, table env_ac_ainah_r2.[1][2]

Demand Range researches industrial accounts in Ireland, the UK and the EU. This page covers the national, sector-level view of manufacturing greenhouse gas emissions: how much each EU country's manufacturing emits, which manufacturing sectors emit most, and how Ireland's official accounts compare with the EPA's national inventory. It does not list plants or installations. For those, see our pages on EU ETS installations and the European Industrial Emissions Portal.

01 / key facts

Key facts

Eurostat env_ac_ainah_r2 and env_ac_aeint_r2, greenhouse gases in CO2 equivalent, 2024; EPA provisional inventory, 2025; checked 24 November 2026.

  • EU-27 manufacturing (NACE Rev. 2 section C) emitted 688,442.0 thousand tonnes (kt) of CO2 equivalent in 2024, 26.2% of the 2,625,704.4 kt emitted by all NACE activities, by our count. Both EU cells are flagged imputed (I).[1][2]
  • That was 0.2% less than in 2023 and 17.0% less than in 2015, by our count.[2][3]
  • Germany's manufacturing emitted the most, 173,147.1 kt (I), or 25.2% of the EU-27 total by our count. Germany, Italy, France, Spain and Poland together accounted for 65.2%, by our count.[2]
  • Four sectors produced 78.7% of EU-27 manufacturing emissions in 2024, by our count: other non-metallic mineral products (which includes cement), basic metals, chemicals, and coke and refined petroleum products.[2]
  • Ireland's manufacturing emitted 6,723.9 kt in 2024 (flagged estimated, E), 1.0% of the EU-27 total and 11.1% of Irish production emissions, by our count. Other non-metallic mineral products alone were 46.8% of it.[2]
  • The EPA puts Ireland's manufacturing combustion and industrial processes at 6.00 million tonnes CO2eq in 2025, or 11.4% of national emissions (provisional).[6]

Three things stand out, by our count:

  1. Size and share are different rankings. Germany emits the most in tonnes, but manufacturing's share of national production emissions is highest in Austria (44.5%), Slovakia (44.2%) and Belgium (38.8%). Countries where other activities, such as power or agriculture, dominate the total show a lower manufacturing share.
  2. Ireland sits low on both. Ireland ranks 18th of 27 in tonnes, and its 11.1% share is the third lowest after Malta (1.2%) and Denmark (5.7%).
  3. Most countries barely moved in 2024. The EU-27 total fell 0.2% on 2023. Romania (+11.3%) and Lithuania (+7.4%) rose most; Croatia (−14.7%) and Latvia (−10.4%) fell most. With most 2024 cells imputed or estimated, small year-on-year changes may be revised.

If you count households as well, manufacturing was 20.8% of all EU-27 greenhouse gas emissions recorded in the accounts in 2024 (688,442.0 kt of 3,305,380.0 kt, both flagged (I)), by our count. So manufacturing is about a quarter of EU-27 emissions from production activities and about a fifth once households are included, by our count.[2]

The EU-27 pattern is heavy process industry. Non-metallic minerals, basic metals, chemicals and refining together made up 78.7% of manufacturing emissions, by our count. Food and drink was a distant fifth at 8.0%.[2]

Ireland's mix is narrower. Non-metallic minerals (46.8%), food and drink (18.3%) and basic metals (17.6%) made up 82.6% of Irish manufacturing emissions in 2024, by our count. Chemicals, the third-largest EU emitter, was only 1.7% in Ireland, while pharmaceuticals was 4.2%, against 0.7% EU-wide. All Irish 2024 sector cells are flagged estimated (E); the 2023 cells are unflagged.[2]

04 / ireland eurostat accounts and the epa inventory

Ireland: Eurostat accounts and the EPA inventory

Two official sources describe Irish manufacturing emissions, and they give different numbers for good reasons.

  • Eurostat air emissions accounts: Irish manufacturing emitted 6,723.9 kt CO2 equivalent in 2024 (E), down from 6,781.2 kt in 2023 and 7,224.1 kt in 2022. That is 6.9% below 2022 and 11.5% below the 2015–2024 high of 7,599.5 kt in 2021, by our count.[2][3]
  • EPA provisional inventory: "Emissions relating to Manufacturing Combustion and Industrial Processes combined accounted for 11.4% of Ireland’s total emissions in 2025, or 6.00 Mt CO2eq." Manufacturing combustion was 4.162 Mt in 2025, down 3.9%, and industrial processes 1.834 Mt, down 1.8%. In 2024 the two lines totalled 6.199 Mt, by our count. Total national emissions in 2025, excluding land use, were 52.65 Mt CO2eq.[6]

Why the totals differ: Eurostat's metadata explains that air emissions accounts "refer to the residents of the reporting country (so-called residence principle as established in national accounts)", while national inventories under the UN climate convention refer to the territory of the country. The accounts assign emissions to the industry that produces them; the inventory groups them by source. For example, the EPA counts oil refining under Energy Industries, while Eurostat counts it in manufacturing as coke and refined petroleum products (C19, 315.1 kt in 2024 (E)).[5][6]

Cement features in both sources. The EPA says total emissions from the cement sector "amounted to 2.20 Mt CO2eq, or 4.2% of national total emissions" in 2025. In Eurostat's accounts, the sector that includes cement, other non-metallic mineral products, emitted 3,145.1 kt in 2024 (E), the largest of Ireland's 19 manufacturing sector groups.[2][6]

Use the EPA figure when you need Ireland's latest year and its climate-target framing. Use Eurostat when you need to compare Ireland with other EU countries or split manufacturing by NACE sector, because the accounts use the same industry codes as business statistics.

UK comparison (ONS). Eurostat's env_ac_ainah_r2 has no UK rows for 2022 to 2024, so UK figures come from a separate publication: the Office for National Statistics (ONS) atmospheric emissions accounts, greenhouse gases by industry, released 28 October 2026. UK manufacturing (SIC 2007 section C) emitted 64,076 kt CO2 equivalent in 2024, 4.9% less than the 67,380 kt of 2023, by our count. That was 18.1% of UK emissions from production activities, excluding households (64,076 of 353,901 kt), by our count, against 26.2% for the EU-27 and 11.1% for Ireland in the same year. The 2025 figure is provisional: 57,882 kt, 9.7% below 2024 and 16.6% of UK production emissions, by our count.[7][8]

In 2024 the largest UK manufacturing emitters were coke and refined petroleum products (18.6% of UK manufacturing emissions), other non-metallic mineral products (16.2%), basic metals (11.4%), chemicals (9.8%) and food products (9.5%), by our count of the ONS industry groups. ONS suppresses two groups (SIC 14 and SIC 31) as low reliability, and publishes no group detail for 2025.[8]

The ONS tables are on a "UK residence basis". ONS says its accounts start from the UK's National Atmospheric Emissions Inventory, and "The residence principle is then applied to these datasets, thereby apportioning the emissions to an industrial classification based on the Standard Industrial Classification: SIC 2007." Eurostat's accounts use the same residence principle, so by our reading the shares can be compared in broad terms, but the UK figures are a separate national publication. They are not in the EU country table above, and UK numbers should be cited to ONS, not Eurostat.[5][8]

05 / emissions per euro of output and its limits

Emissions per euro of output, and its limits

Eurostat's env_ac_aeint_r2 divides emissions by economic output. Against gross value added in current prices, EU-27 manufacturing emitted 266.73 grams of CO2 equivalent per euro in 2024 (I). Ireland's figure was 40.41 grams (E), the lowest in the EU by our count, followed by Malta (61.85, I) and Denmark (65.78, I). Cyprus (1,033.54, I), Greece (758.64, I) and Bulgaria (676.37, I) were highest.[4]

Against total output (production value) rather than value added, EU-27 manufacturing emitted 79.94 grams per euro in 2024 (I) and Ireland 20.68 grams (E).[4]

Read these ratios with care:

  1. The denominator moves as much as the tonnes. The ratio falls whenever value added rises, even if a plant's emissions do not change. So a low ratio can reflect high value added per tonne as much as low emissions. Read the ratio next to the tonnes in the country table.
  2. Sector mix drives the national average. A country with a large cement or steel base tends to show a high ratio even if each plant is efficient. Compare like with like by sector before drawing conclusions.
  3. Current prices include inflation. For trends over time, Eurostat also publishes the ratio in chain-linked volumes (2020) and as an index (2015=100) in the same table.[4]
  4. Estimated and imputed cells. Ireland's 2024 ratio is flagged estimated (E) and most other countries' are imputed (I).[4]
06 / using sector emissions in account research

Using sector emissions in account research

These national tables do not name any company, but they tell you which countries and sectors hold the tonnes. That is a starting point for sizing demand for decarbonisation equipment and services. Five practical uses:

  1. Size the market before you list accounts. If you sell process-heat, kiln, furnace or carbon-capture equipment, the four heavy sectors hold 78.7% of EU-27 manufacturing emissions, by our count. In Ireland, three sectors hold 82.6%.
  2. Pick countries by sector, not by total. Germany has the most manufacturing emissions, but manufacturing is a larger share of production emissions in Austria, Slovakia and Belgium, by our count.
  3. Move from sector to site. Use EU ETS installation data for verified installation-level emissions, the European Industrial Emissions Portal for facility-level pollutant releases, and the UK industrial emissions registers for UK sites.
  4. Pair emissions with energy use. Emissions show where carbon sits; industrial energy use by EU country shows where fuel and power demand sits. The two together help separate combustion-heavy sectors from process-emission sectors such as cement.
  5. Look for dated triggers in Ireland. Sector totals do not change fast enough to time outreach. For dated, site-level signals, see energy audit signals for industrial suppliers.

07 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

What share of EU greenhouse gas emissions comes from manufacturing?
In 2024, EU-27 manufacturing emitted 688,442.0 kt of CO2 equivalent, 26.2% of emissions from all production activities, by our count of Eurostat env_ac_ainah_r2. Including households, the share was 20.8%, by our count. Both EU totals are flagged imputed by Eurostat, so they may be revised in later releases.[2]
Which EU country has the highest manufacturing emissions?
Germany, with 173,147.1 kt of CO2 equivalent in 2024 (imputed), or 25.2% of the EU-27 manufacturing total, by our count of Eurostat env_ac_ainah_r2. Italy, France, Spain and Poland followed. Together, those five countries accounted for 65.2% of EU-27 manufacturing greenhouse gas emissions in 2024, by our count.[2]
How much do Irish manufacturers emit?
Eurostat's accounts put Irish manufacturing at 6,723.9 kt of CO2 equivalent in 2024, an estimate, which is 11.1% of Irish production emissions by our count. The EPA's provisional inventory puts manufacturing combustion and industrial processes at 6.00 Mt CO2eq in 2025, or 11.4% of national emissions.[2][6]
Why do Eurostat and the EPA give different figures for Ireland?
They use different methods. Eurostat's air emissions accounts follow the residence principle and assign emissions to the industry that produces them. The EPA inventory follows the territory principle and groups emissions by source, so oil refining sits under Energy Industries rather than manufacturing. Use Eurostat to compare countries and the EPA for Ireland's latest year.[5][6]
Can I use these figures to find target accounts?
Not directly. env_ac_ainah_r2 gives national totals by sector, not plants or company names. Use it to decide which countries and sectors to prioritise, then move to installation-level sources such as EU ETS data and the European Industrial Emissions Portal, and to company-level research, to build a named account list.[1][2]
