Irish manufacturing productivity: what the official data shows

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 24 November 2026

In Q2 2026, seasonally adjusted labour productivity was €510.6 per hour in Manufacturing – Foreign and €81.4 per hour in Manufacturing – Domestic. Those are the short labels the CSO uses in its Q2 2026 release for two groups of manufacturing sectors, not a split of individual enterprises. The two series moved in opposite directions that quarter: productivity rose 26.4% in the foreign-dominated group and fell 8.0% in the domestic-dominated group. The CSO called the 26.4% rise the largest growth in labour productivity in Q2 2026; by our count it ranked first among the 19 sectors in the seasonally adjusted table. The rates are log growth rates, quarter on quarter.[1][2]

Demand Range researches industrial accounts in Ireland, the UK and the EU. This page owns the CSO's quarterly output-per-hour figures for Irish manufacturing, and what they can and cannot tell a supplier. For manufacturing's share of gross value added across EU countries, see EU manufacturing GVA by country. For account research on Irish manufacturers, see Irish manufacturing account research.

01 / key facts

Key facts

CSO, Productivity in Ireland Quarter 2 2026, tables PIQ02 and PIQ01; checked 24 November 2026.

  • Seasonally adjusted GVA per hour in Q2 2026 was €510.6 in Manufacturing – Foreign and €81.4 in Manufacturing – Domestic (PIQ02). By our count, that is about 6.3 times as much output per hour in the foreign-dominated group as in the domestic-dominated group.[1]
  • Manufacturing – Foreign labour productivity rose 26.4% quarter on quarter, made up of GVA growth of 22.1% and a 4.3% drop in hours worked, according to the CSO. Manufacturing – Domestic productivity fell 8.0%, made up of GVA growth of 4.4% and a 12.4% rise in hours worked. All of those rates are seasonally adjusted log growth rates.[1][2]
  • Year on year, not seasonally adjusted (PIQ01), labour productivity in Q2 2026 was 12.4% lower than in Q2 2025 in Manufacturing – Domestic, and 10.7% higher in Manufacturing – Foreign.[4]
  • The CSO publishes this as a Frontier Series release and says particular care must be taken when interpreting the statistics. There is no official all-manufacturing total in PIQ01 or PIQ02.[1][2][4]

By our count, €510.6 is about 6.3 times €81.4. For context, seasonally adjusted labour productivity in the total economy (NACE A–T) was €118.4 per hour in Q2 2026 (the CSO release writes €118.40). That total-economy figure is not a manufacturing-only measure. In the same quarter the wider Domestic sector of the economy stood at €59.0 per hour and the wider Foreign sector at €519.9 (the release writes €59 and €519.90).[1][2]

The latest quarter in the tables is Q2 2026. Q3 2026 is not yet published. The CSO released these figures on 19 November 2026.[1][2]

03 / foreign dominated and domestic sectors

Foreign-dominated and domestic sectors

In this release, "Foreign" means sectors where foreign-owned multinational enterprise turnover is, on average, more than 85% of the sector total. All other sectors are "Domestic" (also called Domestic & Other). That is a classification of sectors, not a productivity figure for individual enterprises.[2][3]

The Q2 2026 release uses the short labels Manufacturing – Foreign and Manufacturing – Domestic. The PIQ01, PIQ02 and PIQ03 tables list the same two groups under longer official names:

  • Manufacturing – Foreign is manufacturing sectors dominated by foreign-owned multinational enterprises (NACE 19–21, 26 and 31–32): coke and refined petroleum, chemicals, pharmaceuticals, computer, electronic and optical products, furniture and other manufacturing.[1][3]
  • Manufacturing – Domestic is manufacturing sectors dominated by domestic and other enterprises (NACE 10–18, 22–25, 27–30 and 33): food and the other manufacturing classes not in the foreign-dominated group, including metals, machinery and electrical equipment.[1][3]

The wider Foreign sector of the economy also includes publishing, telecommunications, information technology services and rental and leasing (NACE 58, 61, 62–63 and 77). NACE 77 is classified as Foreign in this productivity release, but not in other CSO National Accounts publications. Those service activities are not part of Manufacturing – Foreign.[3]

In Q2 2026, seasonally adjusted labour productivity in Manufacturing – Foreign rose 26.4% on the previous quarter. The CSO says this was "the largest growth in labour productivity in Q2 2026" and that it "was made up of growth in GVA of 22.1% and a drop in hours worked of 4.3%." In Manufacturing – Domestic, productivity fell 8.0% (the release writes "a fall in productivity in Manufacturing – Domestic of 8%"), "made up of an increase in GVA of 4.4% and an increase in hours worked of 12.4%." All of those rates are official, seasonally adjusted, log growth rates from PIQ02.[1][2]

Series (CSO label) Labour productivity GVA Hours worked
Manufacturing – Foreign +26.4% +22.1% −4.3%
Manufacturing – Domestic −8.0% +4.4% +12.4%

Source: CSO PIQ02, seasonally adjusted log growth rates, Q2 2026 compared with Q1 2026. Last updated 19 November 2026 at 11:00 Irish time, UTC+0; checked 24 November 2026. The CSO release quotes the same decompositions.[1][2]

The two groups also differ in scale. In Q2 2026, not seasonally adjusted, Manufacturing – Domestic worked 65.1 million hours and produced €5,650.5 million of GVA at constant prices, while Manufacturing – Foreign worked 74.3 million hours and produced €37,389.3 million, according to PIQ01. Adding those official cells gives 139.4 million hours and €43,039.8 million, by our count. The domestic-dominated group accounted for 46.7% of that hours sum, about 47%, and 13.1% of that GVA sum, about 13%. Those sums are not an official all-manufacturing total, and they should not be turned into an implied all-manufacturing figure for output per hour. Hours in the seasonally adjusted table are different and must not be mixed with these cells. The CSO also notes that the two seasonally adjusted manufacturing GVA series will not exactly match seasonally adjusted manufacturing GVA in the Quarterly National Accounts.[3][4]

04 / why the two series diverge

Why the two series diverge

The same-quarter contrast is hours versus GVA. In Manufacturing – Foreign, hours fell and GVA rose, so productivity rose. In Manufacturing – Domestic, both hours and GVA rose, but hours rose faster, so productivity fell. The CSO's background notes state that the log growth rate of labour productivity is the difference between the log growth rates of GVA and hours worked, which is why 22.1 minus −4.3 equals 26.4, and 4.4 minus 12.4 equals −8.0.[2][3]

The CSO says that, because of "the considerable influence of the Foreign sector on productivity measures for the total economy, this release prioritises the Domestic sector for both presentational and analytical purposes."[2]

Year on year, using the not seasonally adjusted table (PIQ01), the picture in Q2 2026 was in the same direction as the quarterly move.

Series (CSO label) Labour productivity GVA Hours worked
Manufacturing – Foreign +10.7% +7.2% −3.5%
Manufacturing – Domestic −12.4% −6.7% +5.7%

Source: CSO PIQ01, not seasonally adjusted log growth rates, Q2 2026 compared with Q2 2025. Last updated 19 November 2026 at 11:00 Irish time, UTC+0; checked 24 November 2026.[4]

GVA in Manufacturing – Domestic was lower year on year in each of the four quarters from Q3 2025 to Q2 2026, by our count of the official cells: −6.5% in Q3 2025, −8.2% in Q4 2025, −8.6% in Q1 2026 and −6.7% in Q2 2026. Hours worked in that group were higher year on year in three of those four quarters.[4]

The foreign-dominated manufacturing series is volatile. In Q1 2026, not seasonally adjusted, its GVA was 51.0% lower than in Q1 2025 and its labour productivity was 56.3% lower (log growth). That is why a single quarter should not be read as a trend. The CSO also notes that movements in productivity should generally be viewed over a longer time period, even though these results provide the most up-to-date picture of productivity in the Irish economy.[2][4]

05 / what productivity can and cannot tell a supplier

What productivity can and cannot tell a supplier

These figures can show how output per hour has moved in two groups of Irish manufacturing sectors, and whether a change came from GVA, from hours, or from both. They can also sit beside the CSO's unit labour cost series, which compares hourly employee compensation with real labour productivity. They cannot tell you who specifies or buys equipment in a plant, and they are not a factory-floor efficiency measure. Hours worked come from the Labour Force Survey and include employees, the self-employed and hours in secondary jobs. A high or low euro-per-hour figure for a sector group is not a signal to approach, or avoid, a particular site.[2][3]

The CSO publishes this as a Frontier Series output and warns: "Particular care must be taken when interpreting the statistics in this release as it may use new methods which are under development and/or data sources which may be incomplete, for example, new administrative data sources."[5] All growth rates in the release are log growth rates, not ordinary percentage changes. The background notes say the two are close when growth is small, and that they diverge when growth is large, which is relevant to the foreign-dominated series. Ordinary percentage changes computed from the euro-per-hour levels are not the same as the published log growth rates. The results in the release are seasonally adjusted unless otherwise stated. Use PIQ02 for quarter-on-quarter rates and for the headline euro-per-hour levels, and PIQ01 for year-on-year rates.[2][3][1][4]

In Manufacturing – Domestic, unit labour costs were 17.2% higher in Q2 2026 than in Q2 2025, not seasonally adjusted, according to PIQ01. Hourly compensation rose 4.9%. In Manufacturing – Foreign, unit labour costs were 2.3% higher. The CSO describes unit labour cost as a competitiveness indicator: growth suggests that the cost of labour is rising relative to labour productivity. It is not a buying-role or margin figure. Quarter on quarter, the seasonally adjusted unit labour cost series can move differently: in Q2 2026 it fell 1.1% in Manufacturing – Domestic and 19.7% in Manufacturing – Foreign.[3][4][1]

There is no official all-manufacturing (NACE C) total in PIQ01 or PIQ02. Foreign direct investment in Irish manufacturing is a related, separate question; see IDA FDI investment account signals. For manufacturing GVA across the EU, see EU manufacturing GVA by country. For how to research individual Irish manufacturing accounts, see Irish manufacturing account research.

06 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

How productive is Irish manufacturing?
In Q2 2026, seasonally adjusted labour productivity was €510.6 per hour in Manufacturing – Foreign and €81.4 per hour in Manufacturing – Domestic, according to the CSO. That is a split of sectors, not of individual enterprises. Manufacturing – Foreign covers sectors where foreign-owned multinational turnover averages more than 85% of the sector total.[1][2][3]
Is Irish manufacturing productivity falling?
Not in both series. Seasonally adjusted labour productivity fell 8.0% quarter on quarter in Manufacturing – Domestic in Q2 2026 and rose 26.4% in Manufacturing – Foreign, according to the CSO. Year on year, not seasonally adjusted, the domestic-dominated group was 12.4% lower than in Q2 2025, with GVA lower in all four quarters to Q2 2026, by our count.[1][2][4]
Why do the two series move in opposite directions?
In Q2 2026, hours fell and GVA rose in Manufacturing – Foreign, so productivity rose. In Manufacturing – Domestic, hours rose faster than GVA, so productivity fell. The foreign-dominated series is volatile: in Q1 2026, not seasonally adjusted, its labour productivity was 56.3% lower than in Q1 2025. The CSO therefore prioritises the Domestic sector in the release.[2][4]
Are labour costs rising in Irish manufacturing?
In Manufacturing – Domestic, unit labour costs were 17.2% higher in Q2 2026 than in Q2 2025, not seasonally adjusted, according to the CSO. Hourly compensation rose 4.9%. In Manufacturing – Foreign, unit labour costs were 2.3% higher. Unit labour cost compares hourly employee compensation with real labour productivity. It is a competitiveness indicator, not a buying-role or margin figure.[3][4]
How should these CSO productivity figures be read?
With care. The CSO publishes this as a Frontier Series release and says particular care must be taken when interpreting the statistics. All growth rates in the release are log growth rates. Results are seasonally adjusted unless otherwise stated. Use PIQ02 for quarter-on-quarter changes and levels, and PIQ01 for year-on-year rates.[1][2][3][4]
Is there an official all-manufacturing productivity total?
No. The CSO tables list the foreign-dominated and domestic manufacturing groups separately, with no combined manufacturing (NACE C) total. Any hours or GVA sum of the two groups on this page is by our count of those official cells, not a CSO manufacturing total. The two seasonally adjusted GVA series will not exactly match Quarterly National Accounts manufacturing GVA.[1][3][4]
