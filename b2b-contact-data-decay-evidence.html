How fast does B2B contact data go out of date?

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 10 November 2026

There is no official, single rate at which B2B contact data goes out of date. The figures most often repeated — 22.5% a year and 70.3% a year — are vendor claims. This page traces them to the pages that still carry them, and then sets out an official labour-market proxy that can inform list-maintenance advice without being treated as a decay rate for any one list.

Demand Range does evidence-backed industrial account research for Ireland, the UK and the EU, and is based in Ireland. Named contacts and outreach are separate scopes, not included by default. This is a research guide, not legal, marketing or data-protection advice, and not a measurement of a particular database.

01 / key facts

Key facts

Eurostat's experimental dataset lfsi_long_e07 publishes job-to-job transitions as annual averages of quarterly estimated probabilities, rounded to full percentage points, for people aged 15-74.[1][2][3] The dataset was last updated on 11 June 2026. We extracted it on 10 November 2026.

  • European Union (27 countries, from 2020): 3% in 2024 and 2% in 2025 (total, aged 15-74).[1][2]
  • Ireland: 3% in 2024 and 3% in 2025 (total, aged 15-74).[1][2]
  • United Kingdom: 2% in 2020, the last year published for the UK in this dataset. Values for 2021 to 2025 are not published.[1][2]
  • Eurostat describes the indicator as the probability that a person employed in both the initial and the target quarter started a new work contract between the two interviews.[4]
  • These official cells are a labour-market proxy. They are not a measured decay rate for B2B contact records, email addresses or prospect lists.

Key facts checked against the sources below on 10 November 2026.

02 / where the 22 5 a year figure comes from

Where the 22.5% a year figure comes from

HubSpot's Database Decay Simulation, accessed on 10 November 2026, has no visible publication date. It states: "Email marketing databases naturally degrade by about 22.5% every year*."[6] A sources note on the same page says: "Marketing Sherpas research shows that B2B data decays at a rate of 2.1% per month. This is an annualized rate of 22.5%."[6] The page also lists HubSpot's 2013 State of Inbound Marketing Report and 2014 State of Inbound Report. It does not give a year, sample size or method for the MarketingSherpa research, and it does not link to that research.[6]

When we checked on 10 November 2026, we did not find a MarketingSherpa page that publishes the method, year or sample behind that figure. We are not claiming that no such study ever existed. We are saying that the figure, as it is still presented, has no published method we could check. Do not treat 22.5% a year as an official rate, and do not use it as a Demand Range rate.

03 / where the 70 3 a year figure comes from

Where the 70.3% a year figure comes from

A Biznology article dated 13 February 2015, accessed on 10 November 2026, says: "In 2009, we updated our original 2002 study on B2B Data Decay"[7]. It then states that "the study showed that on average B2B data decays at a rate of just over 5% per month, or 70.3% per year."[7] The method described is a self-reported business-card survey: "the results of over 1,200 cards handed in by individuals who participated in the survey. They were simply asked what has changed on their business card in the last 12 months."[7] The article calls this "self-reported data".[7]

Among the field results it lists are a 65.8% "title and/or job function change", 29.6% of individuals who "changed companies (new job)", and a 37.3% "email address change".[7] The article does not show how the 70.3% headline was calculated from those field results. This is a vendor-described survey from 2002 and 2009. Do not use 70.3% as a current official rate, and do not apply it as a decay rate for an industrial account list in Ireland, the UK or the EU.

04 / official labour market proxies

Official labour-market proxies

For list-maintenance advice, use official statistics as context rather than importing a vendor percentage.

Eurostat publishes dataset lfsi_long_e07 under the title "Job-to-job transitions (annual averages of quarterly transitions, estimated probabilities) - experimental statistics".[1][2] The reference metadata, last updated on 12 June 2026, say that flow statistics "are experimental statistics derived from the longitudinal component of the EU-LFS data".[3] They also say: "They identify the flows between different labour market statuses between consecutive quarters."[3] "The data refer to annual averages of quarterly transitions"; "All results refer to transition probabilities, and are rounded to full percentage points".[3] People used in the calculation "were surveyed in two consecutive quarters and 15-74 years old in both these quarters."[3]

Eurostat's Statistics Explained article on labour market flow statistics (data extracted in September 2026; page last edited on 11 September 2026) describes the job-to-job indicator as "the probability of an individual employed in both the initial and target quarter, having changed jobs in between (defined as starting a new work contract between the two interviews)."[4] That article cites dataset lfsi_long_e07.[4]

The published cells, for the total and the 15-74 age class, are:

Area 2024 2025 Notes
European Union - 27 countries (from 2020) 3% 2% Rounded official cells.[1][2]
Ireland 3% 3% Rounded official cells.[1][2]
United Kingdom not published not published Last published UK cell in this dataset is 2% in 2020.[1][2]

The same Statistics Explained article says that in 2025 the probability "ranged from 5% in Denmark and the Netherlands to just 1% in Bulgaria, Czechia, Greece, Italy, Poland and Romania."[4] Those 2025 cells match the dataset extracted on 10 November 2026.[2] The article's 2025 comparison leaves out Luxembourg, Malta, Slovakia and Bosnia and Herzegovina. The Figure 5 note says: "The size of the underlying sample does not allow the publication of probabilities for Luxembourg, Malta, Slovakia, and Bosnia and Herzegovina."[4] In every country in that comparison, the article says the rate for people aged 15-24 was much higher than for older workers, with the lowest rate for those aged 55-74.[4]

Do not multiply a quarterly probability by four and present the product as an official annual rate. Eurostat does not publish that annualisation in this dataset. The figures are already "annual averages of quarterly transitions".[1][3] Leave them in the form Eurostat publishes.

05 / what those official figures do not tell you

What those official figures do not tell you

A job-to-job transition probability is not a test of a stored email address, a job title, a telephone field or a company record. It does not tell you whether a mailbox still accepts mail, whether a person was promoted in the same contract, or whether a company has opened, closed or changed activity.

Company openings and closures are a different official question, covered by enterprise birth and death statistics. This page does not republish that country table. Use the job-to-job series here for contact-level labour-market context, and keep company-list churn as a separate check.

The UK cannot be read from this series after 2020. Do not fill the gap with an EU or Irish figure, and do not invent a UK rate.

06 / how to use this when you maintain a list

How to use this when you maintain a list

Use official proxies to explain why a list ages. Do not paste 22.5% or 70.3% into a forecast, a service page or a sales claim.

Keep company fit and contact details as separate questions. A person starting a new work contract can make a named contact unusable while the company remains a valid account. A company that has closed can make the account a removal even if a mailbox still works.

When you already hold a bought, inherited or internally built list, test it against current evidence rather than assuming a headline decay rate. The B2B prospect list audit covers a one-off check of a list you already hold. The industrial prospect list refresh covers triggers, cadence and retain, hold, correct or remove decisions over the life of a list. If you are choosing between a data-provider extract and researched accounts, see an industrial prospect list compared with a data provider.

If you want Demand Range to run an account-level review, request a written scope that states your range, territory and exclusions. Keep personal contact details out of the enquiry unless a separate contacts scope has been agreed.

07 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

How fast does B2B contact data go out of date?
There is no single official decay rate for B2B contact records. The percentages most often repeated online are vendor claims, not statistical-office measurements. As a labour-market proxy, Eurostat's experimental job-to-job series shows a 3% quarterly transition probability for Ireland in 2024 and 2025, and 3% then 2% for the EU-27. That is not a measured email-list decay rate.[1][2][3][4]
Is the 22.5% a year data-decay figure real?
It is a real vendor claim, but not an official or current rate. HubSpot's Database Decay Simulation says email marketing databases "naturally degrade by about 22.5% every year" and attributes a 2.1% monthly rate to MarketingSherpa research, annualized to 22.5%. The page gives no year, sample or method, and we did not find a MarketingSherpa page that publishes one.[6]
Where does the 70.3% a year figure come from?
A February 2015 Biznology article reports a self-reported business-card survey first run in 2002 and updated in 2009, covering more than 1,200 cards. People were asked what had changed on their card in the last 12 months. The article states "just over 5% per month, or 70.3% per year". This is a vendor-described survey, not an official rate.[7]
What official figures can I use instead?
Use Eurostat's experimental job-to-job probabilities in dataset lfsi_long_e07. They are annual averages of quarterly estimated probabilities, rounded to full percentage points, for people aged 15-74 employed in both quarters who started a new work contract between interviews. In 2025 they were 2% in the EU-27 and 3% in Ireland. The UK series in this dataset ends in 2020.[1][2][3][4]
Does a 3% job-to-job rate mean my contact list decays at 3% a quarter?
No. The Eurostat figure is a labour-market transition probability, not a test of your records. It does not measure title changes in the same job, abandoned mailboxes, or list-hygiene errors. Test a list you already hold, and refresh accounts when company, site or brief evidence changes.[3][4]
