CSRD and suppliers: who still reports, and what they can ask you for

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 24 November 2026

After the Omnibus I directive, mandatory CSRD reporting is limited to companies and groups with more than €450m net turnover and more than 1,000 employees on average. From financial years starting on or after 1 January 2027, a supplier with 1,000 or fewer employees can decline CSRD data requests that go beyond a fixed list of datapoints: 23 for firms with more than 10 employees, and 9 for firms with 10 or fewer, by our count of Annex II.[1][2]

Demand Range researches industrial accounts in Ireland, the UK and the EU. This page owns one question: what a company that reports under the EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) can require from an industrial supplier, quoted from the published law and dated. It summarises the law as published; it is not legal advice.

01 / key facts

Key facts

Directive (EU) 2026/470; Delegated Regulations (EU) 2026/1560 and 2026/1563; Irish Statute Book; checked 24 November 2026.

  • Mandatory reporting applies to undertakings, and parent undertakings of groups on a consolidated basis, that exceed both a net turnover of €450m and an average of 1,000 employees during the financial year.[1]
  • Directive (EU) 2026/470 ("Omnibus I") was published in the Official Journal on 26 February 2026. Member States must transpose its CSRD parts by 19 March 2027.[1]
  • A "protected undertaking" is one in a reporter's value chain with no more than an average of 1,000 employees during the preceding financial year. It may decline CSRD requests for information beyond the voluntary standard.[1]
  • The value-chain cap is set by Annex II of Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/1560 and applies from financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2027.[2]
  • On 24 November 2026, the Irish Statute Book's 2026 index listed statutory instruments numbered up to S.I. No. 588, none with "sustainability" in its title, by our search. The Department of Enterprise, Tourism and Employment told the Dáil on 10 November 2026 that transposition work was in progress.[7][9]
02 / who still reports under csrd after omnibus i

Who still reports under CSRD after Omnibus I

Omnibus I amends the CSRD and the accounting directive it sits in. The new size test has two limits, and a company must exceed both: a net turnover above €450m and an average of more than 1,000 employees during the financial year. For a parent company, the test runs on the group's consolidated figures.[1]

The directive entered into force on the twentieth day after publication, which is 18 March 2026 by our count. The same text dates the Commission's new delegated powers from 18 March 2026.[1]

The standards that go with it

The Commission adopted two delegated regulations on 3 July 2026, both published in the Official Journal on 21 September 2026:[2][5][6]

  • Revised European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS), Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/1563: EUR-Lex gives a date of entry into force of 10 November 2026 and a date of application of 1 January 2027. The Commission says the revised standards cut mandatory datapoints by over 60% and total datapoints by over 70%, and expects reporting costs to fall by more than 30% per company. That is the Commission's expectation, not a measured result.[5][6]
  • The voluntary standard, Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/1560: a reporting standard for companies outside CSRD's scope, which also sets the value-chain cap. It is based on the standard in Commission Recommendation (EU) 2025/1710, which Omnibus I links to the voluntary standard for SMEs (VSME) developed by EFRAG.[1][2]

The due-diligence directive (CSDDD) is a separate regime. Omnibus I raises its thresholds to more than 5,000 employees and €1.5bn net turnover, and the value-chain cap does not cover due-diligence requests.[1]

03 / the value chain cap for smaller suppliers

The value-chain cap for smaller suppliers

Omnibus I gives smaller companies in a reporter's value chain a statutory right to refuse CSRD data requests beyond a set limit. The directive calls them "protected undertakings": companies that do not exceed an average of 1,000 employees during the preceding financial year and are in the value chain of a reporting company.[1]

What the directive sets out:[1]

  • Right to decline: protected undertakings "shall have the right to decline to provide information exceeding the information specified in the voluntary standards" when the request is made for CSRD reporting.
  • Contracts: a reporter must not use contractual arrangements made for CSRD reporting to require more than the voluntary standard, and a contract term that does so is not binding. The rest of the contract stays binding.
  • Notice: a reporter that asks for more must tell the supplier which information exceeds the voluntary standard, and that it has a statutory right to decline.
  • Self-declaration: a reporter may rely on a supplier's self-declaration that it is a protected undertaking, unless it knows, or can reasonably be expected to know, that the declaration is manifestly incorrect.
  • Compliance: a reporter that leaves out information beyond the cap is deemed to have met its value-chain reporting duty.

The definition sets two tests, size and being in a reporter's value chain. It does not say where the supplier must be established.[1]

What the cap does not cover

The cap applies only to information gathered for CSRD reporting. The directive's recitals say it does not stop voluntary sharing, such as information commonly shared in a sector, and does not affect due diligence or information gathered for other purposes, such as the reporter's risk management.[1]

The Commission's value-chain Q&A of 6 May 2026, written while the voluntary standard was still in draft, puts it this way: reporters "cannot require" companies with 1,000 employees or fewer to provide information beyond the cap, but may still request it. In that case they "must clearly indicate which requested information exceeds the value chain cap" and say that the supplier has a statutory right to decline. The Q&A adds that reporters should ask only for what they need.[3]

Banks, insurers and other financial institutions are "still encouraged", not required, to limit their requests to smaller companies for purposes beyond CSRD reporting to the voluntary standard, according to the recitals of Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/1560.[2]

Annex II of Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/1560 lists every datapoint covered by the cap. By our count it has 23 datapoint rows for suppliers with more than 10 employees, and 9 for suppliers with 10 employees or fewer. The cap applies from financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2027.[2]

Energy and emissions: what the GHG row covers

The emissions row points to paragraph 33 of the voluntary standard. It asks for estimated gross GHG emissions in tonnes of CO2 equivalent, considering the GHG Protocol Corporate Standard (2004), including Scope 1 emissions and location-based Scope 2 emissions. Paragraph 33 is marked voluntary for undertakings with 10 employees or fewer.[2]

Annex II has no Scope 3 row, and, by our reading of the table, no environmental row for suppliers with 10 employees or fewer. So for a CSRD request, the most a reporter can require from an industrial supplier with 11 to 1,000 employees is its energy use in MWh, Scope 1 and location-based Scope 2 emissions, water withdrawal and waste, plus the general-information, circular-economy, business-description, workforce, business-conduct and incident datapoints in the table.[2]

05 / where ireland stands on transposition

Where Ireland stands on transposition

Ireland transposed the original CSRD in 2024, through the European Union (Corporate Sustainability Reporting) Regulations 2024 (S.I. No. 336/2024). S.I. No. 309/2025, given under official seal on 7 July 2025, gave effect to the EU's "Stop the Clock" directive, which moved later reporting dates back by two years.[4][8][10]

Omnibus I's CSRD changes need a new Irish statutory instrument. As of 24 November 2026:

  • Not yet in the Statute Book, by our search. The Irish Statute Book's 2026 index listed statutory instruments numbered up to S.I. No. 588, none with "sustainability" in its title. The index can lag recent signings.[9]
  • 10 November 2026 written answer: the Department "is progressing work on the transposition of the CSRD-related elements of the Omnibus I Directive in advance of the 19 March 2027 transposition deadline". It added that Ireland "intends to avail of the Member State option provided for in Article 3(1)(c)", which allows the new scope thresholds to apply early for certain existing first-wave companies.[7]
  • 9 June 2026 written answer: the statutory instrument "is expected" to be signed before the end of 2026.[8]
  • The Department's CSRD page still describes Omnibus as being negotiated, so that part has not been updated since the directive was adopted.[4]

Irish companies that have already reported

IAASA, the Irish audit and accounting supervisor, says it was made aware of 20 entities in scope for the first wave of CSRD that had to publish their first sustainability statements during 2025. First-wave entities are public-interest entities with more than 500 employees. At 1 December 2025, 42 individuals and 11 audit firms were approved to carry out sustainability assurance in Ireland.[11]

Using CSRD scope in account research

CSRD scope tells a supplier which customers may send sustainability data requests, and what those requests can cover. It is not a published list of firms, and the official size statistics stop short of the CSRD line.

Eurostat's top size class is 250 or more persons employed, so the number of Irish manufacturers above the 1,000-employee line is not published. CSRD also tests groups on consolidated figures, so none of these counts is a count of firms in scope.[1][12]

Ways to use the rules in account research:

  • Expect a capped request. For a supplier with 11 to 1,000 employees, Annex II points to energy in MWh, Scope 1 and location-based Scope 2 emissions, water and waste. Our reading is that, for CSRD reporting, the most a customer can require on climate is a company-level energy total in MWh and estimated Scope 1 and location-based Scope 2 emissions, not Scope 3 data; anything beyond that can be asked for but may be declined. That is an inference from the table, not a published finding.[2][3]
  • Find plants with published emissions data. Emissions-trading registry files list installations with verified emissions. See our guide to EU ETS installations as account signals and our EU Industrial Emissions Portal research guide.
  • Look for energy-audit evidence. Mandatory energy audits and published action plans can show where metering and efficiency work is planned. See our energy-audit signals for industrial suppliers.
  • Score timing separately from fit. A customer's first CSRD year is a timing signal, not proof of a need. Weight it with fit and evidence in our industrial account prioritisation score.

07 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

Does CSRD still apply to my manufacturing company?
Only if your company, or its group on a consolidated basis, exceeds both €450m net turnover and an average of 1,000 employees. Omnibus I set that test for financial years starting on or after 1 January 2027. Smaller first-wave reporters fall out of scope, and Member States may exempt them for financial years starting in 2025 and 2026.[1]
What can a CSRD customer require from a supplier with 1,000 or fewer employees?
For its CSRD report, no more than the datapoints in Annex II of Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/1560: 23 for suppliers with more than 10 employees and 9 for those with 10 or fewer, by our count. The cap covers energy, emissions, water, waste and workforce items, and applies from financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2027.[1][2]
Can a CSRD customer still ask for more than the cap?
Yes, it can ask, but it cannot require it. A reporter that requests information beyond the voluntary standard must say which items exceed the cap and that the supplier has a statutory right to decline. A contract term that requires more for CSRD reporting is not binding, while the rest of the contract stands.[1][3]
Do suppliers have to provide Scope 3 emissions under the cap?
No. The emissions row in Annex II covers estimated Scope 1 and location-based Scope 2 emissions, considering the GHG Protocol Corporate Standard. There is no Scope 3 row, and suppliers with 10 employees or fewer cannot be required to give any emissions figure. A customer can still ask for more, which the supplier may decline.[2]
Has Ireland transposed Omnibus I?
Not as of 24 November 2026, by our search of the Irish Statute Book's 2026 index. On 10 November 2026 the Department of Enterprise, Tourism and Employment said transposition work was in progress ahead of the 19 March 2027 deadline, and that Ireland intends to use the option to apply the new thresholds early for certain first-wave companies.[7][9]
Does the cap cover due diligence or bank requests?
No. The value-chain cap covers only information gathered for CSRD reporting. It does not affect due-diligence duties or requests made for other purposes, such as risk management. Banks and other financial institutions are encouraged, but not required, to keep their requests to smaller companies within the voluntary standard.[1][2]
