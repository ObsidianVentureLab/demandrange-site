Irish planning applications as industrial project signals

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 10 November 2026

A planning application is the earliest public signal of a new or extended industrial site in Ireland. It names the applicant and the location, but it rarely names the equipment. It shows a proposal has been lodged, not that a project will proceed. Public routes are the national dataset on data.gov.ie, each planning authority's file, and An Coimisiún Pleanála.

This guide is for suppliers of pumps, valves, process equipment, water treatment and mechanical services. It explains where Irish planning data is published, what a record can support, and how to add a signal to an account record without treating a rumour as a project.

01 / why a planning application is a signal not a project

Why a planning application is a signal, not a project

New or extended industrial buildings in Ireland generally need planning permission before work starts. Local Government Ireland says planning permission is required for any development or building works not considered “exempted development”, and that if permission is needed “you cannot start building until planning permission has been granted.”[4]

The application names the applicant and the site, and describes the development. Drawings and reports in the planning-authority file often show plant rooms, tanks and process buildings. They rarely name the equipment, the brand or the contractor who will buy it. A lodged application is public evidence of a proposal. A grant is public evidence of permission. Neither, on its own, is evidence that work has started, that a package is available, or that your equipment is in the design.

Do not add a rumoured, uncited or unofficially described project to an account record. Only record what a cited public file actually states, at the stage it supports, with the date you checked it.

02 / route 1 the national planning applications dataset

Route 1: the national planning applications dataset

The dataset on data.gov.ie is published by the Department of Housing, Local Government and Heritage and licensed under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.[1] The catalogue record, opened on 10 November 2026, states these values exactly:[1][2]

  • Date released: 2017-10-06
  • Update frequency: Weekly
  • Period of time covered (begin): 2010-01-01
  • Geographic coverage: National
  • Metadata last modified: 22 September 2026, 08:18 UTC

The catalogue metadata date is not a data-refresh date. The same day, the planning-application points map service behind the dataset reported a data last-edit time of 10 November 2026 at 12:26 UTC.[3] Treat those two dates separately. By our count, the catalogue's coverage start is not uniform: on 10 November 2026, 26 of the 31 planning authorities had no records received before 2017, while three authorities had 101,276 records received before 2010, the earliest on 5 March 1991.[3] Check the date range for the authority you search before you compare years.

The dataset offers an Esri REST service and GeoJSON files for application points and site polygons.[1] Fields on the points layer include:[3]

  • planning authority and application number
  • development description and development address
  • application type and status
  • received, decision, grant and expiry dates
  • decision, further-information request dates and appeal details
  • land-use code, site area and floor area
  • a link to the application on the authority's own system
  • applicant forename, surname and address

By our count, a query of the points layer on 10 November 2026 returned 511,965 application records from 31 planning authorities. In that query, the newest received date was 6 November 2026.[3] The latest received date still differed between authorities, so check how recent each authority's records are before you rely on an extract.

The applicant-name fields can include private individuals. Record the company, where one is named, and the application number. Do not store names of private individuals for account research.

03 / route 2 the planning authority file

Route 2: the planning authority file

The full file for each application sits with the planning authority. The dataset's link field takes you to it.[3] Many local authorities also accept applications through Planning.LocalGov.ie; Local Government Ireland says this facility will eventually be rolled out to every local authority in Ireland.[4]

On the authority portal you can usually see the drawings, reports, submissions, further-information requests and the decision. These are where plant evidence, if it exists, is most often found: plant-room layouts, tank sizes, drainage and water connections, and noise or emissions reports that describe the installation.

Key timings, as set out by Local Government Ireland (page last updated 22 September 2026):[4]

  • Before applying, the applicant must put up a site notice and, for a significant application, may need a newspaper notice. The application must be submitted within two weeks of those notices.
  • Anyone can make a submission or observation for five weeks from the date the application is received.
  • The planning authority generally must decide within 8 weeks of receiving the application, but it may take longer if more information is needed. The applicant then has 6 months to respond to a further-information request.
  • Anyone who made a submission or observation has four weeks to appeal a decision to An Coimisiún Pleanála.
  • Permission normally remains valid for five years, unless another timescale is granted. Outline permission remains valid for three years.

For suppliers, two stages are especially useful to record: a further-information request, because the design may still be changing, and a grant, because permission then exists. A grant is still not a start-on-site date and not a purchase.

04 / route 3 an coimisi n plean la for strategic cases and appeals

Route 3: An Coimisiún Pleanála for strategic cases and appeals

If a development is very large or complex, such as strategic infrastructure development, Local Government Ireland says the application may need to be lodged with An Coimisiún Pleanála rather than with the local planning authority.[4]

Strategic infrastructure development (SID) applications are submitted directly to An Coimisiún Pleanála for major infrastructure. Its examples include “motorways, railway lines, pipelines, airports, ports, or major facilities like hospitals.”[5] The SID process includes a pre-application consultation stage. The public are informed about those consultations but cannot take part in them.[6]

An Coimisiún Pleanála also hears appeals on local authority decisions. Its case search covers cases from 2016 to date, and you can search by details such as date, address or planning authority, with a separate search for cases from 1995 to 2015.[7] For an industrial proposal that has been appealed, the appeal file can hold a detailed public description of the development. It still does not, by itself, confirm equipment, a contractor or a buying route.

05 / paid project databases in brief

Paid project databases, in brief

Some suppliers buy researched project data instead of reading planning files themselves. This page does not describe or compare those products.

Public planning files remain the citable record of what was actually applied for and decided. A paid database entry is only as strong as the evidence it cites.

06 / a method from application to account

A method: from application to account

  1. Define the signal. Write down the development types that could use your equipment, such as process buildings, extensions, energy centres or treatment works. Keep this as a relevance rule, not a claim that every such application will buy from you.
  2. Search the national dataset. Filter by planning authority and by words in the development description. Note the application number and the received date, and check how recent that authority's records are.[1][3]
  3. Open the planning-authority file. Read the drawings and reports for plant, tanks, water and drainage. Quote only what the file states.
  4. Record the stage and the date. Lodged, further information, granted, refused or under appeal. A later rumour does not move the stage.
  5. Link the site to the company. Check the applicant against company registers and environmental licences. Our guide to Irish environmental register evidence shows how a licence can later confirm a site.
  6. Add it to the account record. Note the evidence, the open questions and the next check date. Our opportunity intelligence work follows this pattern: record what the source supports, keep inferences separate, and leave unknowns open.

Water and wastewater connections often appear in the same file. For utility-side programme context, see Irish water investment research.

07 / a worked example wholly fictional

A worked example (wholly fictional)

The supplier, company, site, application and all statements below are invented. No actual planning application, grant, company or equipment purchase is represented.

An invented flow-control supplier filters the national points dataset for one planning authority and the word “extension”. It finds an application by “Fictional Process Operator C” for a process-building extension with a new boiler house and an effluent treatment plant.

  • Planning-authority file: drawings show a new process hall, two silos and a treatment-plant upgrade. No brand or contractor is named.
  • Stage: further information has been requested on noise, and a response has been lodged. That is not a grant and not a purchase.
  • Account record: process valves, effluent valves and steam valves are plausible applications. The buyer route is unknown. The design team is named in the reports.
  • Next check: the decision date, then — if permission is granted — the grant date plus four weeks for any appeal.

If permission is later granted, record the grant and the expiry date. Do not treat the grant as work on site, and do not invent a package from the drawings.

08 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

Where can I download Irish planning applications?
From the national planning applications dataset on data.gov.ie, published by the Department of Housing, Local Government and Heritage.[1] The catalogue lists Weekly updates and a coverage start of 1 January 2010.[1][2] By our count, 26 of the 31 planning authorities have no records received before 2017.[3] Use the Esri REST or GeoJSON files, then open the linked authority file.[1]
How often is the national dataset updated?
The data.gov.ie catalogue lists the update frequency as Weekly.[1] On 10 November 2026 that record showed metadata last modified on 22 September 2026, not a refresh date.[2] That day, the points map service reported a last-edit time of 10 November 2026 at 12:26 UTC.[3] The latest received date still varied by authority, so check it before relying on an extract.[3]
Does a planning application list the equipment to be bought?
Rarely. The record names the applicant and the site and describes the development. The planning-authority file may include drawings and reports that show plant rooms, tanks, or water and drainage connections.[1][4] Those documents seldom name brands, contractors or a buying route. Treat the file as a research signal, not a confirmed order or proof that the project will be built.
Which cases go to An Coimisiún Pleanála instead of the local authority?
Strategic infrastructure development applications go directly to An Coimisiún Pleanála for major infrastructure. Its examples include “motorways, railway lines, pipelines, airports, ports, or major facilities like hospitals.”[5] It also hears appeals; anyone who made a submission or observation has four weeks to appeal.[4] Its case search covers cases from 2016 to date, with a separate search for 1995 to 2015.[7]
Is a planning application a confirmed industrial project?
No. A lodged application is evidence that a proposal has been filed. A grant is evidence that permission has been given, not that work has started or that equipment will be bought.[4] Permission normally remains valid for five years, and outline permission for three years.[4] Record the stage and the date. Do not add a rumoured or uncited project.
