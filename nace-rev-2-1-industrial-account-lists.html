NACE Rev. 2.1 manufacturing codes: what changed and how to fix your industrial account lists

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 28 October 2026

NACE Rev. 2.1 manufacturing codes mostly match the old ones, but the classes that moved can quietly break an industrial account list. NACE Rev. 2.1 is the EU's updated activity classification, phased into official statistics from 2025 to 2031. Map old codes with Eurostat's correspondence tables, then review the changed classes by hand.

Key facts (EU Regulation 2023/137 and Eurostat code lists; checked 28 October 2026)

  • NACE Rev. 2.1 is set by Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/137 and is being phased into official statistics from 1 January 2025 through to 2031, according to Eurostat's methodology.[1][2]
  • In Eurostat's published code lists, manufacturing (Section C) has 24 divisions, 92 groups and 232 classes in NACE Rev. 2.1, against 24 divisions, 95 groups and 230 classes in NACE Rev. 2.[6][7]
  • Class 25.61 was treatment and coating of metals in NACE Rev. 2 and is manufacture of cutlery in NACE Rev. 2.1, while new class 28.97 covers manufacture of additive manufacturing machinery.[6][7]

01 / 1 how section c is structured now

1. How Section C is structured now

In Eurostat's published code lists, manufacturing (Section C) keeps 24 divisions. It has 92 groups and 232 classes in NACE Rev. 2.1, against 95 groups and 230 classes in NACE Rev. 2.[6][7] The counts barely move, but codes inside them change meaning. Class 25.61, for example, was treatment and coating of metals in Rev. 2 and is cutlery in Rev. 2.1.[6][7]

02 / 2 section c changes that matter for industrial suppliers

2. Section C changes that matter for industrial suppliers

Regulation (EU) 2023/137 sets NACE Rev. 2.1 and its 22 sections, and Eurostat's methodology describes the phase-in from 2025 to 2031.[1][2] Eurostat's correspondence tables map old codes to new ones.[3]

The table below is a summary; the Eurostat methodology document is the authoritative source.[2]

16.1 (sawmilling) Split into 16.11 and 16.12 Decide which part fits your ICP
20.51 Now manufacture of liquid biofuels (in Rev. 2, explosives); explosives, glues and essential oils moved to 20.59[3] Re-check chemical sub-segments
22.23 (plastic builders' ware) Split into 22.23 (doors and windows of plastic), 22.24 (builders' ware of plastic) and 22.25 (processing and finishing), with part moving to 23.66[3][7] Remap plastics targets
25.21 (central heating radiators and boilers) Merged with 25.30 Combine the two lists
Group 25.6 (treatment and coating of metals; machining) Restructured as group 25.5: 25.51 coating, 25.52 heat treatment, 25.53 machining. In Rev. 2.1, 25.61 is cutlery and 25.62 is locks and hinges Review every machining and coating account
26.8 (magnetic and optical media) Merged into 26.7 Minor for most industrial lists

03 / 3 a safe recoding process

3. A safe recoding process

  1. Export your account list with the current NACE Rev. 2 or UK SIC 2007 code on each row.
  2. Join it to Eurostat's NACE Rev. 2 → Rev. 2.1 correspondence table.[3]
  3. Flag one-to-many rows, where one old class maps to several new ones. These need a human decision, usually from the company's website or its register filing.
  4. Keep both codes during the transition. National registers and statistics move at different speeds; Germany, for example, runs WZ 2025 alongside WZ 2008.[4]
  5. Re-run your ideal customer profile filters against the new codes, and compare account counts before and after.

Fictional example: A fictional coatings supplier targets NACE Rev. 2 class 25.61 (treatment and coating of metals). Under NACE Rev. 2.1 that work sits in 25.51 (coating of metals) or 25.52 (heat treatment of metals), and machining moves from 25.62 to 25.53.[2] In Rev. 2.1, 25.61 and 25.62 mean cutlery and locks and hinges, so a filter left on the old codes would pull in the wrong companies. The supplier reviews each one by hand, keeps both codes in its CRM, and updates its ICP filter so no accounts are silently dropped. This example is fictional and only illustrates the method.

04 / 4 uk and irish lists

4. UK and Irish lists

  • UK: Companies House records use UK SIC 2007, which is derived from NACE Rev. 2.[5] Don't assume a UK code matches a NACE Rev. 2.1 code.
  • Ireland: check which NACE version each dataset uses. For the codes that matter in Irish lists, see our Irish manufacturing NACE codes page; for the Irish manufacturing base, see our Irish manufacturing account research guide.
05 / 5 codes are a starting point not a list

5. Codes are a starting point, not a list

Activity codes are self-declared and often out of date. Use them to build a first cut. Then confirm each account with evidence: products on the website, permits (see the EU Industrial Emissions Portal guide), tenders and hiring. Our industrial ideal customer profile and prospect list maintenance guides cover that step.

06 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

When does NACE Rev. 2.1 apply?

It is being phased into official statistics from 1 January 2025 through to 2031.[1][2] National versions such as Germany's WZ 2025 run alongside the old codes during the switch.[4]

Do all manufacturing codes change?

No. Section C keeps 24 divisions, and most classes are unchanged. A small number were split, merged or renumbered, which is why one-to-many mappings need a manual check.[3][6][7]

Is there an official mapping from NACE Rev. 2 to Rev. 2.1?

Yes. Eurostat publishes correspondence tables between the two versions.[3]

Do UK SIC codes follow NACE Rev. 2.1?

Not automatically. UK SIC 2007 is derived from NACE Rev. 2, so map UK codes separately.[5]
