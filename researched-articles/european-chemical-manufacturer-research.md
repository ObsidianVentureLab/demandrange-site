# European chemical manufacturer research: find the operation behind the label

**Do not filter a chemical account list on “manufacturer” alone.** A supplier researching mixing equipment, valves, seals or instrumentation needs to know what work happens at the proposed customer's site. A substance producer, a blending operation and an own-brand chemicals distributor can need very different research questions—even when their websites sell similar products.

The useful output is an **operation-fit note**: which company performs which chemical activity, at which site, and why that activity belongs in your brief. Keep equipment suitability and any current purchase separate. A formulator can be an excellent account to investigate without being a manufacturer in the REACH sense; an importer label is not evidence of a production plant.

## Separate three meanings of manufacturer

This guide is for research into operating sites in **named EU countries**, not an undifferentiated “Europe” list. ECHA's manufacturer definition uses the European Economic Area (EEA); that territory includes the EU plus Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway.[1][5] If your commercial brief is EU-only, do not silently add those other EEA countries. Assess other European territories separately. This is commercial research guidance, not legal advice, a REACH status assessment or equipment-selection guidance.

**REACH role.** ECHA describes a manufacturer as an EEA-based individual or company that produces or extracts a chemical substance. Its guidance explicitly distinguishes someone who only blends substances into mixtures: that activity falls under downstream use, rather than manufacture in this definition.[1]

**Formulation activity.** ECHA's downstream-user guidance describes formulators as producing mixtures, with paints, adhesives and cleaning agents as examples.[2] Do not turn “downstream user” into “not a production prospect”. For a supplier whose brief concerns blending operations, the performed activity is the relevant commercial test. The regulatory term neither qualifies nor disqualifies the account by itself.

**Statistical activity.** Eurostat's NACE Rev. 2.1 explanatory notes include custom resin mixing and blending in class 20.16, paints and varnishes in 20.30, and detergent preparations in 20.41.[4] These are activity-classification examples, not a list of installed processes at every company carrying a chemical-sector code. Record the classification version you encountered; do not translate a code into a plant description or silently relabel older records as Rev. 2.1.

Keep these meanings in separate fields. “Manufactures cleaning products” in a company description, a NACE heading and “manufacturer under REACH” are not interchangeable evidence. Nor does the absence of the regulatory manufacturer label establish an absence of industrial operations.

## Test the distributor label without discarding a formulator

ECHA's distributor guidance describes sourcing a substance or mixture within the EEA, storing it and placing it on the market for someone else without changing its chemical composition; that can include sale under the distributor's own brand.[3] An own-brand catalogue therefore does not resolve who performs the production.

The same guidance distinguishes buying from outside the EEA and placing the chemicals straight on the EEA market as an importer situation. Mixing, diluting or refilling chemicals bought within the EEA before supplying them to others is described instead as downstream use.[3] ECHA also identifies re-fillers as transferring substances or mixtures between containers, generally for repackaging or rebranding.[2]

For account research, use those distinctions to ask **what the business actually does**, not to assign a legal status from a sentence. A trading company may describe more than one operation. Do not assume every location blends, or that an organisation described as a distributor has no other relevant activity.

For a blending-focused brief, separate these observations:

- **An identified site produces mixtures:** investigate the stated formulation work and the operator-to-site connection. Do not assume that every formulation involves the same process.
- **An identified site only transfers finished product into other containers:** assess a filling or handling brief if relevant; this does not establish blending.
- **A catalogue or label identifies a supplier:** hold the operating-site claim until there is separate evidence of where and by whom the work is performed.
- **A source expressly describes resale with no qualifying activity at the candidate site:** exclude that site from the production brief for that stated reason, not because distributors are commercially unimportant.

An unresolved description is a hold, not proof that production is absent. A different supplier brief can legitimately lead to a different decision.

## Map the operation before proposing an equipment fit

Write a short chain for the specific site: **material received → work performed → output supplied**. Fill only the links supported by the source. “Chemical solutions” does not tell you whether the company synthesises a substance, blends purchased ingredients, repacks finished product or arranges production elsewhere.

Start with attributable first-party site and capability descriptions. Where appropriate and permitted, use a site-specific public process document to clarify the activity. Record whether it describes an operating activity, an available capability, a proposal or historical work. A permit, registration or generic process reference must not be promoted into proof of present operation.

The following are our research prompts, not predictions about machinery:

<dl>
<dt>Evidence describes substance production or extraction</dt>
<dd>Record the actual process words and their site context. Ask which step is relevant to the supplier's range; do not infer a complete reaction, separation or transfer system from the substance name.</dd>
<dt>Evidence describes formulation or blending</dt>
<dd>Establish whether the named operator performs the work at this site. Retain any stated mixing, dispersion or dilution step separately; do not add a step merely because it seems typical of the product family.</dd>
<dt>Evidence describes filling or repackaging</dt>
<dd>Ask whether the material arrives as a finished mixture and what work occurs before it leaves. A container change is not proof of formulation, but can still matter under a separately agreed handling-equipment brief.</dd>
<dt>Evidence describes a brand with outsourced production</dt>
<dd>Keep the brand and the performing organisation separate. Seek the actual production relationship if the scope permits; do not assign the contractor's plant or installed assets to the brand owner.</dd>
</dl>

Then write the supplier connection as a question. For example: “Does the evidenced batch-blending operation fall within our mixing-equipment research scope?” Do not replace it with “this plant needs a new mixer”. For a component range, “does the identified operation involve an application we can assess?” comes before any assertion about a specific seal, valve or instrument.

Product names, regulatory status and a process description do not supply fluid properties, duty conditions, materials compatibility, installed equipment or technical approval. Leave those engineering fields unassessed until supported by appropriate evidence and review. A relevant operating account also does not establish current procurement, an accessible package or authority to select suppliers. Use the [pump and flow-control guide](pump-flow-control-prospecting.html) if the unresolved question is the customer route rather than chemical activity.

## Work a supplier decision with fictional evidence

**Everything in this example is invented:** the supplier, companies, sites, evidence cards and outcomes. It is not an anonymised customer case, a verified prospect list or an engineering recommendation. Identity, permitted use and commercial-exclusion checks are stipulated as resolved solely for the exercise. No live source dates or contact details exist.

**Fixed brief:** an invented mixing-equipment supplier wants companies operating industrial cleaning-mixture blending in Belgium or the Netherlands. The required activity is on-site blending of purchased ingredients. Pure resale, refill-only operations and brand-only businesses outsourcing all blending are outside this particular brief. Count companies, retaining the qualifying site and activity beneath each. No active project is required.

### Candidate A: retain the formulator, not every address

Fictional Formulation A's invented site card describes its Belgian operation receiving purchased ingredients, blending cleaning mixtures in batches and then filling containers. A separate invented card identifies its Dutch location as a sales office. A group summary calls A a downstream user.

**Decision: retain A for the defined account-research shortlist**, linked to the Belgian blending operation. The downstream-user wording is not a reason to reject it. The sales office is not another production site, and the filling step does not erase the separately stated blending activity.

The handover says: “The stipulated Belgian activity meets the blending requirement. Installed mixer design, operating conditions, renewal need and supplier-selection route are unknown. Next, establish whether the described application is within the supplier's technical assessment scope.” No legal-status conclusion is required to reach this commercial decision.

### Candidate B: reject refill-only for this brief

Fictional Chemical Supply B imports finished cleaning mixtures, transfers them into smaller containers at its Dutch operation and sells them under its own label. The invented activity card expressly says no blending takes place there.

**Decision: exclude B from this blending brief.** The decisive evidence is the stated absence of the required operation, not its importer label. Do not describe B as having no equipment or no potential value. A filling-equipment supplier could propose a different brief, but cannot change this one retrospectively.

### Candidate C: hold the operation, not the chemistry keyword

Fictional Cleaning Brand C lists a Belgian address and a catalogue of formulated products. Its invented wording says “our European production network” but identifies neither the performing entity nor a blending site.

**Decision: hold C.** Ask for an attributable description connecting a named operator, the required activity and a site in the selected territory. More product pages do not close that gap. If the next permitted source expressly establishes that all blending is outsourced and the candidate is brand-only, exclude C under the current brief; investigate a named producer only as a separate candidate with its own checks.

**Counterfactual:** remove A's site card, leaving only the downstream-user label. A becomes a hold too. The regulatory role cannot replace the operation evidence that made the first decision possible.

## Copy this operation-fit worksheet into your brief

Use one note for one company–site–activity combination. Link additional qualifying operations to the same company record rather than counting each note as a new account. This is an original manual worksheet, not an official classification or automated acceptance test.

- **Brief and boundary:** supplier range; required performed activity; named countries; exclusions; company or facility counting unit.
- **Identity and place:** company reference; site reference; evidenced operator; site country. Distinguish office, warehouse and operating location only to the extent supported.
- **Three separate descriptions:** commercial wording; any attributed regulatory-role wording; statistical activity code and version if used. Write “not assessed” where no reliable basis exists.
- **Operation chain:** material received; work performed; output supplied. Attach an evidence reference to each supported link; leave missing links unknown.
- **Source and permission:** exact URL or document reference; supporting passage; stated publication/update date or “not stated”; actual check date; source-use conditions for the intended output.
- **Phase and limits:** current operation, capability, proposed or historical description; conflicts; what the source does not establish.
- **Supplier-fit question:** the specific connection to investigate. Keep installed assets, duty, compatibility, current need and purchasing responsibility separate.
- **Decision and next check:** retain, hold or exclude against the fixed brief; reason; the missing fact that could change it; where the bounded review will stop if unresolved.

Before handover, check: **is the decision supported by an operation at an attributed site, or merely by a regulatory or product label?** Retain account fit independently of any project-signal status. “Project research not required” is not “no project exists”.

## Bring the activity question to a research scope

A useful enquiry is: “We supply [range] and want company-level research into [performed chemical activity] in [named EU countries]. Include formulation if it meets that activity test; exclude [specified non-matches]. Record the operator, qualifying site, evidence and unresolved application questions. Do not infer equipment demand from registration or sector labels.”

Compare the [fictional account-research sample](sample-account-research.html) with the worksheet, or review the [industrial account-research service](industrial-lead-research.html). Coverage, source availability, language needs, fields and commercial terms require assessment and a written scope. Do not send confidential customer records or personal contact data in an initial enquiry; named-contact delivery and outreach are separate scopes.

## What the sources support and where they stop

- **ECHA role guidance:** the manufacturer/downstream-user distinction, formulator and re-filler examples, and the distributor/importer distinction.[1][2][3] It does not identify a researched plant or establish any individual company's status here.
- **Eurostat's 2025 NACE Rev. 2.1 manual:** the selected activity examples in classes 20.16, 20.30 and 20.41, printed pages 136–137.[4] It does not describe a particular site's process or provide a market-size estimate.
- **EUR-Lex's EEA glossary:** the territory distinction used at the start of the guide.[5] It does not define the territory of a Demand Range assignment.
