How to use EU State aid awards as industrial signals

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 25 November 2026

The European Commission's Transparency Award Module (TAM) publishes individual State aid awards so that the beneficiary's name, amount, location, sector and objective can be seen. It is a useful investment signal for industrial suppliers, but it is not a complete list of every award in the EU, and this page does not give an award count. In most cases Member States must publish awards exceeding EUR 100 000, generally within six months of granting. A separate Commission publication, the State aid Scoreboard, puts EU-27 expenditure in 2024 at EUR 168.23 billion, or 0.94% of GDP. That Scoreboard total is context only: it is not a sum of the awards published in TAM.[1][2][5]

Demand Range researches industrial accounts in Ireland, the UK and the EU. This page is a guide to searching TAM and using an above-threshold award as an account signal. It does not list recipients, and it is not legal advice. For Irish inward-investment announcements, see how to read IDA Ireland investment announcements as account signals. For how to score a timing signal, see industrial account prioritisation.

01 / key facts

Key facts

European Commission Transparency Award Module and State aid Scoreboard 2025; checked 24 November 2026.

  • TAM "gives access to state aid individual award data provided by Member States in compliance with the European transparency requirements for state aid." A typical record includes the beneficiary's name and identifier, whether the enterprise was an SME or large at the time of granting, the sector at NACE group level, the aid element in national currency, the aid instrument, the date of granting, the objective, the granting authority and the reference of the aid measure. Records also include a NUTS level II regional code; this page discusses awards at country level only.[1][2]
  • In most cases, Member States must publish all individual awards exceeding EUR 100 000. Some legal bases set a different threshold, so the legal basis of the measure should be checked. Transparency information must in general be published within six months of granting the aid. For tax advantages, Member States have up to one year from the submission of the relevant tax declaration. The State aid Modernisation transparency requirements have been mandatory since 2016.[2]
  • TAM is not a complete census of aid. Member States may publish transparency information on TAM or on a comprehensive State aid website at national or regional level, and some awards appear only on those national sites. Awards for financial products supported by the InvestEU fund under Section 16 of the General Block Exemption Regulation (GBER) are not in the public search. Awards below the relevant threshold, and de minimis aid (which sits in a different register), are also outside TAM's published set.[1][2][5]
  • This page does not give a count of TAM awards, or of manufacturing awards. No official total of that kind was verified on 24 November 2026.[1]
  • Separately, the State aid Scoreboard 2025 (published 15 January 2026) puts EU-27 State aid expenditure in 2024 at EUR 168.23 billion, or 0.94% of EU-27 GDP. The Scoreboard measures the aid element reported by Member States, not the number or value of awards in TAM, and it excludes de minimis aid. Do not add Scoreboard totals to TAM award amounts.[5]
  • Ireland and Portugal had the lowest Member State ratios in 2024, around 0.4% of national GDP. Ireland's ratio rose from 0.38% of GDP in 2023 to 0.40% in 2024.[5]
  • Environmental protection and energy savings was the largest objective in the Scoreboard, at EUR 68.82 billion in 2024, about 45% of non-crisis aid (EUR 151.90 billion), by our count, and 7% higher than in 2023.[5]
02 / what the transparency award module publishes

What the Transparency Award Module publishes

The Commission's public search is the official Union-level window on individual awards that Member States have notified under the transparency rules. The Commission states that it is meant so that citizens and companies can see the beneficiary's name, amount, location, sector and objective.[1][2][3]

The fields typically published, as listed on the Commission's aid-beneficiaries page, are:[2]

  • name of the beneficiary
  • beneficiary's identifier
  • type of enterprise (SME or large) at the time of granting
  • region at NUTS level II
  • sector of activity at NACE group level
  • aid element, in national currency
  • aid instrument
  • date of granting
  • objective of the aid
  • granting authority
  • where applicable, an entrusted entity and selected financial intermediaries
  • the reference of the aid measure

The Commission cites Article 9(1)(c) and Annex III of Commission Regulation (EU) No 651/2014 (the GBER), together with the transparency sections of other legal bases, as the legal hook for those fields.[2]

The aid element in a TAM record is the official published amount of aid, in national currency. It is not, by itself, a description of the firm's total project cost, and it is not a purchase order for a supplier.

A related Commission tool, the competition case search, lists authorised aid measures. It is not an award list, and a case record should not be treated as evidence that a named firm received a payment.[7]

03 / thresholds deadlines and what tam does not cover

Thresholds, deadlines and what TAM does not cover

Use these official rules when you decide whether a missing name means "no aid" or only "not published here".[2]

  • Threshold. In most cases, the publication duty applies to individual awards exceeding EUR 100 000. Always check the legal basis for the measure in front of you; some bases set a different threshold.
  • Ordinary deadline. In general, transparency information must be published within six months of granting the aid. A record you search for today may therefore relate to an award granted up to six months earlier, or it may still be unpublished.
  • Tax advantages. For tax advantages, Member States have up to one year from the submission of the relevant tax declaration, so the lag can be longer than six months.
  • Since when. The State aid Modernisation transparency requirements have been mandatory since 2016. The modernisation programme itself was adopted in 2014.

Coverage is incomplete by design, not only because of the lag:[1][2][5]

  • Some Member States publish some or all awards only on a comprehensive national State aid website, not in TAM. TAM itself points to those sites. Do not present a TAM search as a complete list of every aid award in the EU.
  • Awards for financial products supported by the InvestEU fund under Section 16 of the GBER are not in the public search.
  • Awards below the relevant threshold are not in the published set.
  • De minimis aid is published in a different system (a Union-level or national central register), not in TAM. From 1 January 2026, information on general de minimis aid and on aid for services of general economic interest under the de minimis rules must be recorded in a central register at national or Union level; agricultural de minimis follows from 1 January 2027. The Scoreboard also states that its expenditure data do not include funding granted under the de minimis rules.
04 / how to search tam for industrial signals

How to search TAM for industrial signals

Open the Commission's TAM public search. Then:[1][2]

  1. Filter by Member State, for example Ireland, rather than by a place below country level.
  2. Filter by sector of activity at NACE group level. For manufacturing, use section C (groups 10 to 33).
  3. For each result, record the official fields listed above, with the URL and the date you checked it. Leave a field blank when the record does not give it.
  4. Treat the date of granting as the official award date, not the date the record appeared in your search, and allow for the publication lag.
  5. Use the beneficiary's identifier, and the granting-authority and measure references, to confirm the legal entity and the scheme before you attach the record to an account.
  6. If TAM indicates that a Member State publishes some awards only on a national site, follow that pointer and apply the same fields. Keep any written note at country level.

A hit is a signal of public support, and of possible investment, at the firm named in the record. It is not a complete picture of that firm's spending, and it is not evidence that the firm is ready to buy from a particular supplier.

05 / eu 27 state aid expenditure in 2024

EU-27 State aid expenditure in 2024

The figures in this section come from the State aid Scoreboard 2025 note, published on 15 January 2026 and covering Member State expenditure in 2024. The Scoreboard is compiled under Article 6 of Commission Regulation (EC) 794/2004 from annual expenditure reports. Figures are the aid element, not the nominal amount paid, and they exclude most aid to railways, services of general economic interest, and de minimis aid. They are context for how large official aid spending was in 2024. They are not a total of TAM awards, and they should not be added to TAM award amounts.[4][5][6]

Aid for EU priorities EUR 151.90 billion (about 90%), 0.85% of EU-27 GDP
Crisis-related aid EUR 16.33 billion (about 10%), 0.09% of EU-27 GDP; 67% lower than the previous year

Active GBER measures 6,509 measures, 69% of all active measures (compared with 41% in 2014)

The largest nominal spenders in 2024 were Germany, at EUR 41.37 billion (about 25% of the EU-27 total); France, at EUR 34.18 billion (20%); and Italy, at EUR 18.34 billion (11%). Ireland and Portugal had the lowest ratios to national GDP, around 0.4%. Ireland rose from 0.38% of GDP in 2023 to 0.40% in 2024.[5]

Total Scoreboard expenditure in 2024 was 17% lower than in 2023, after a 22% fall in 2023 compared with 2022, in the Scoreboard's constant-prices narrative.[5]

These country and objective totals can help a supplier decide where public support is concentrated — for example in environmental protection and energy savings, or in a particular Member State. They do not tell you how many above-threshold awards you will find in TAM, and they do not name recipients.

06 / how to use an award as an account signal

How to use an award as an account signal

Capture the official fields, then decide what research question the record actually supports. A dated award in a manufacturing NACE group can justify checking whether the firm is investing in a new line, energy savings, research, or another project tied to the stated objective. It does not, on its own, show a budget for a particular supplier, a live tender, or a willingness to take a meeting.

Use the award with other dated, public records, not as a stand-alone opportunity:

  • For Irish inward-investment announcements, see how to read IDA Ireland investment announcements as account signals.
  • For EU tender notices, which are a different public record, see TED opportunities for industrial suppliers.
  • When you score timing, treat a confirmed, dated award as one input, not as a reason to make contact. See industrial account prioritisation.

Usual ways the record gets over-read:

  • Absence from TAM is not proof of no aid. The award may be below the threshold, still inside the publication lag, published only on a national site, granted as de minimis, or an InvestEU Section 16 financial-product award.
  • The Scoreboard total is not a TAM total. Expenditure and published awards are different populations and different concepts.
  • The aid element is not the project cost. A large official aid element can sit beside a larger private outlay, or beside work that is already finished.
  • SME or large is a snapshot. The classification is taken at the time of granting and may have changed.
  • A NUTS level II code is not a plant address. Keep research notes at country level unless a separate official source names the site.
  • A case number is not an award. Authorised measures in the case search are not the same thing as a published individual award.

A practical sequence:

  1. Confirm the legal entity from the beneficiary name and identifier.
  2. Record the grant date, aid element, instrument, objective, NACE group and Member State.
  3. Look for a second dated record about the same firm, such as an investment announcement or a tender.
  4. Update the timing score and the evidence note, including the TAM URL and the date checked.
  5. Decide the next research action. Do not move from the award record to outreach.

07 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

What is the Transparency Award Module?
It is the European Commission's public search of individual State aid awards notified by Member States under EU transparency rules. Officially, it "gives access to state aid individual award data provided by Member States in compliance with the European transparency requirements for state aid."[1][2]
What is the publication threshold?
In most cases, Member States must publish all individual awards exceeding EUR 100 000. Some legal bases set a different threshold, so check the legal basis of the measure. The requirement has been mandatory since 2016. Awards at or below the threshold may not appear in TAM.[2]
How soon after an award is it published?
In general, within six months of granting the aid, so a recent award may not appear yet. For tax advantages, Member States have up to one year from the submission of the relevant tax declaration. Some legal bases set different deadlines.[2]
Does TAM list every State aid award in the EU?
No. Some Member States publish some or all awards only on a national State aid website. Awards below the relevant threshold, de minimis aid, and InvestEU Section 16 financial-product awards are also outside the public search. This page does not give an award count, because no official TAM total was verified on 24 November 2026.[1][2]
How does the State aid Scoreboard differ from TAM?
The Scoreboard reports Member State expenditure (the aid element) by year, objective and country. The 2025 edition, published on 15 January 2026, puts EU-27 expenditure in 2024 at EUR 168.23 billion, or 0.94% of GDP. TAM publishes individual above-threshold awards. The two should not be added together or used as substitutes.[5]
