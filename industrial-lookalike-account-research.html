Industrial lookalike account research: find more companies like your best customers

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 28 October 2026

Lookalike account research starts from three to five of your best customers and finds other companies that share the traits that made them good customers. You extract each customer's activity code, size band, regulator evidence and site type, keep only the traits they share, then search public registers for companies with the same traits.

01 / lookalike research and an icp answer different questions

Lookalike research and an ICP answer different questions

An industrial ideal customer profile is a set of rules you write first, then apply. Lookalike research works the other way round: it starts from real customers and works out the rules from what they have in common. Use it when you have a handful of strong customers but no agreed profile, or when you want to test whether your profile matches the customers you actually win.

The output of a lookalike exercise is a short list of shared traits, which then becomes or corrects your ICP. The candidate companies are researched against those traits, not against the customers themselves.

02 / step 1 choose three to five seed customers

Step 1: choose three to five seed customers

Pick customers you would happily win again: accounts that bought for a repeatable reason, not because of a personal introduction, a one-off emergency or an exceptional discount. Three is the minimum for a pattern. More than five usually mixes different customer types.

Work from a generalised description of each seed, inside your own controlled records. Do not upload a customer list, order history or contact details to a worksheet, a search tool or a quote enquiry. A distinctive site or project detail can identify a customer even without its name, so generalise those details too.

03 / step 2 extract the same four traits from every seed

Step 2: extract the same four traits from every seed

Trait What to record Where to check it
Activity code The NACE (Ireland and the EU) or SIC (UK) class of the company Company register filings. Companies House filings use its condensed SIC 2007 list.[3]
Size band Persons engaged, in the same bands that official statistics use Company filings; the CSO publishes Irish counts in bands of under 10, 10 to 19, 20 to 49, 50 to 249 and 250 or more.[1]
Regulator evidence Which register lists the site, and under which activity EPA licences in Ireland;[4][5] the equivalent permit or approval register elsewhere
Site type What physically happens at the site your product serves The licence or permit wording, and the company's own site description

Then compare the seeds. A trait that all or most seeds share is a candidate rule. A trait that only one seed has is a coincidence until proven otherwise. In particular, do not set a size threshold just because one large customer happens to be large.

04 / step 3 turn the shared traits into register searches

Step 3: turn the shared traits into register searches

Each shared trait points to a register you can search.

  • UK companies: the Companies House advanced search filters by SIC code, company status, company type, incorporation date and registered address.[2] Use it to build the first list, then check each site separately, because the registered office is often not the operating site.
  • Irish industrial sites: the EPA licenses specified industrial activities listed in the First Schedule to the EPA Act, and its LEAP portal holds licences and licence documents.[4][5] A licence names the facility and its class of activity, which is often the best evidence of site type. The EPA also publishes a point dataset of industrial emissions facilities as open data, last updated in 2020, so confirm each licence's current status in LEAP.[6] See our guide to Irish environmental register evidence.
  • Size context: in 2024 the CSO counted 19,617 active manufacturing enterprises in Ireland, of which 618 had 50 to 249 persons engaged and 196 had 250 or more.[1] If your seeds are all in the larger bands, that is a universe of about 800 enterprises before any activity filter is applied.

Search results are pointers, not acceptance evidence. Check each candidate against every shared trait before it goes on the list.

05 / worked example a fictional pump seal supplier

Worked example: a fictional pump-seal supplier

Fictional example. A fictional maker of mechanical seals for process pumps picks four seed customers it would gladly win again. It describes them in its own records without names, then extracts the traits.

Seed (fictional) Activity code Size band Regulator evidence Site type
Seed 1 NACE C20.14 (other organic basic chemicals) 50 to 249 EPA industrial emissions licence, chemicals class Continuous process with transfer pumps
Seed 2 NACE C21.10 (basic pharmaceutical products) 250 or more EPA industrial emissions licence, chemicals class Batch active-ingredient production
Seed 3 NACE C20.59 (other chemical products) 50 to 249 EPA industrial emissions licence, chemicals class Solvent recovery and blending
Seed 4 NACE C10.51 (dairies and cheese making) 250 or more EPA industrial emissions licence, food and drink class Milk processing with hygienic pumps

Three of the four seeds share a chemicals licence, 50 or more persons engaged and a process that moves aggressive fluids. Seed 4 shares the size band but not the activity, so the supplier records it as a separate hypothesis for a later food-sector brief. The rule becomes: "EPA-licensed chemical or pharmaceutical process sites in Ireland, run by companies with 50 or more persons engaged." The supplier then searches the EPA licence register for that activity, checks each company's size, and compares each candidate with the rule in the worksheet. Every seed, code choice and decision in this example is fictional and only illustrates the method.

06 / step 4 compare candidates against the rule not the seeds

Step 4: compare candidates against the rule, not the seeds

For each candidate, mark every trait as supported, contradicted, unknown or conflicting, and record the source and the date checked. Then make one of three decisions:

  • Shortlist when every required trait is supported and exclusions are checked.
  • Hold when a required trait is unknown, with the next question written down.
  • Reject when a required trait is contradicted, recording the rule it failed.

Watch for the common false matches: a company that sells equipment for the process rather than running it, a group that lists a sales office in the territory but no production, and a manufacturer that does the right process only on its own products when your rule needs a service provider. The worksheet has a seed-profile column and four blank candidate columns for this comparison. A shortlisted company is a research result, not permission to contact anyone.

07 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

How many seed customers do I need for lookalike research?

Three to five. Fewer than three gives you no pattern to test, and more than five usually mixes different customer types into one profile.

Is lookalike research the same as an ideal customer profile?

No. An ICP is a set of rules you write and apply. Lookalike research works out those rules from real customers, so it is a way to build or correct an ICP.

Can I upload my customer list to find lookalikes?

We do not recommend it. Extract generalised traits from your customers inside your own records, and research candidates against those traits.

Which public registers are best for industrial lookalikes?

Company registers for activity code and size, and environmental or other regulatory registers for site type. In Ireland the EPA licence register is often the strongest site evidence; in the UK, Companies House is the usual starting point for company searches.

Does a lookalike company need an active project?

No. A lookalike is an account that matches your customer profile. Project evidence is a separate question and is not required for an account to be relevant.
