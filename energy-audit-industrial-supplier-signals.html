Research guide · Source evidence and limits

Energy-audit signals for industrial suppliers

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 28 October 2026

An energy audit can justify a research question. It does not show that a business will change pumps, valves or control systems. Keep four things apart: the audit, its findings, the organisation's decision and any later evidence that work was done. This guide shows where audit evidence comes from in Ireland and the EU, and how far it can be taken.

The territory here is the Republic of Ireland, with the EU rules behind it. This is a commercial research guide. It is not an energy audit, an equipment recommendation or legal advice on whether a business must be audited.

01 / who must have an energy audit under eed article 11

Who must have an energy audit under EED Article 11

In the recast Energy Efficiency Directive, Directive (EU) 2023/1791, energy audits for enterprises are in Article 11, "Energy management systems and energy audits".[1] It replaced Article 8 of the earlier Directive 2012/27/EU, and the test is now an enterprise's energy consumption rather than its size.[2]

Average annual energy use (all carriers, previous three years) Obligation under Article 11 First deadline
More than 85 TJ Implement an energy management system, certified by an independent body 11 October 2027
More than 10 TJ, without an energy management system Undergo an energy audit, then at least every four years 11 October 2026

The thresholds and dates are from Article 11(1) and 11(2) of the consolidated text of 5 August 2026.[1] In our own conversion, 10 TJ is about 2.8 GWh and 85 TJ is about 23.6 GWh a year. Member States had to transpose Article 11 by 11 October 2025, with later dates for the first management systems and for first audits by newly covered enterprises.[2]

Annex VI sets the minimum criteria for an audit, including measured, traceable operational data and validated calculations for proposed measures.[1] A sustainability headline is not that analysis.

Article 11(2) also requires a concrete and feasible Action Plan based on the audit recommendations, sent to the enterprise's management. The Action Plan and the recommendation implementation rate must be published in the enterprise's annual report and made publicly available, subject to rules protecting trade and business secrets and confidentiality.[1] The Commission's guidance says this is repeated every year while the obligation applies.[2]

02 / how the obligation is described in ireland

How the obligation is described in Ireland

SEAI runs the Irish Energy Auditing Compliance Scheme. Its page, checked on 28 October 2026, still describes the obligation set by S.I. No. 426 of 2014 and later amendments.[3][8] It lists these tests for an obligated company:[3]

  • 250 or more employees, or
  • annual turnover above €50 million and an annual balance sheet total above €43 million.

Obligated entities have an audit every four years by a Registered Energy Auditor, or use a certified ISO 50001 or ISO 14001 system confirmed by one.[3]

The audit must cover at least 85% of the organisation's delivered energy use in the Republic of Ireland, and the company must notify SEAI.[3] The same page names "Leap to 11", a project among eleven national energy agencies to support the transposition of Article 11.[3]

We have not confirmed how the Article 11 energy-use thresholds have been written into Irish law. Check SEAI's current guidance before relying on either test. For research, the point is simpler: an obligated company has a recurring audit, but SEAI's page describes a notification to SEAI, not publication of the findings.[3] What Article 11(2) makes public is the Action Plan and implementation rate.[1]

03 / seai s large industry energy network large energy users not projects

SEAI's Large Industry Energy Network: large energy users, not projects

The Large Industry Energy Network (LIEN) is SEAI's network for large energy users. Members get an energy advisor, special working groups, training and events. A company can join if it holds or is pursuing ISO 50001 certification, or if it spends more than €1 million a year on energy.[4]

SEAI publishes a member list and says LIEN has more than 200 active member companies.[5] Its results page reports that, in early 2025, 192 companies reported on their 2024 performance. Their final energy use was 23.9 TWh, about 16.6% of the national total. 154 companies reported verified energy savings of 386 GWh.[6] Food and drink has the most members, and PharmaChem the second most.[6]

How to use it in account research:

  • As fit context. Membership is public evidence that a company sees itself as a large energy user working on energy management. It supports an account-fit note. It is not an audit finding.
  • At site level. The list includes offices, hotels, banks and data centres as well as process plants.[5] Check the site and its activity against your brief.
  • By linking, not copying. Link to SEAI's list and record the date checked. Do not present it as your own prospect list.

LIEN membership tells you nothing about a planned project, a budget or a supplier choice.

04 / classify the statement before interpreting it

Classify the statement before interpreting it

Public statement you might find Record only what is shown Keep these questions open
Listed as a LIEN member SEAI lists the company as a member on the date checked Which site, and is its activity in your brief?
An audit is planned The organisation says it plans an assessment Has it happened, which site or systems are covered, and what was found?
An audit is completed The organisation reports completion Are relevant findings available and attributable to the target site?
An Action Plan is published The annual report lists audit recommendations and an implementation rate Which measures involve your application, and which are only investigations?
A measure is approved A decision is reported for a defined scope How will it be delivered, who selects suppliers, and what is still uncommitted?
Work is completed A defined intervention is reported as done What evidence supports performance, and is any separate future need established?

These are our working research distinctions, not official audit classifications or a required sequence. Do not fill a later row because an earlier one is evidenced. A group-wide energy target is not a site project, a building-heating review is not a process-pumping review, and "utilities" does not identify a system.

05 / ask for a relevance bridge not a savings promise

Ask for a relevance bridge, not a savings promise

For a pump or flow-control supplier, a defensible research note links what was published to the application:

  1. Attribute the activity. Name the company and site. Is the evidence about its own operations, a customer project or a group programme?
  2. Locate the system. State what is described: a production process, cooling circuit, water system or another defined scope. Leave it unknown if the source does.
  3. Name the change. Separate measurement, operating changes, maintenance, design investigation and physical modification. Do not choose "equipment change" because it suits your range.
  4. Explain the relevance. Say why the scope may warrant investigation. This is a hypothesis, not a technical fit finding.
  5. Keep the decision gaps. Record approval, specification, delivery route, existing arrangements and package status separately.

Do not multiply a site's energy bill by an assumed savings percentage and call it the value of your offer. Do not transfer a savings figure from a case study to another account.

06 / worked example wholly fictional

Worked example — wholly fictional

The supplier, companies, site and all statements below are invented. No real audit, Action Plan, LIEN member or customer result is represented.

A fictional flow-control supplier wants process-production operators in the Republic of Ireland. Its optional project-signal rule needs an evidenced, site-specific proposal affecting a relevant fluid system.

First record: account fit without a qualifying project signal

Fictional Process Operator C passes the brief's identity, activity, territory, exclusion and source-use checks. In the exercise, it appears on the LIEN member list, and a statement says C completed an energy audit, with no findings or system scope.

Decision: include C as an industry-matched account. Record no qualifying signal identified in the bounded review, which is not "no project exists". Membership and the audit are context. Neither meets the fluid-system rule.

Second record: an Action Plan item, not a purchase

C's fictional annual report publishes its Article 11 Action Plan. One item is "investigate control changes to the recirculating cooling-water system at the production site". The plan leaves open whether the outcome is an operating change or an equipment change.

Decision: the item meets the fictional signal rule. Keep the company record and add an opportunity-signal record with the scope, the stage ("investigation") and the report date. Do not label it "pump purchase". Equipment, compatibility and procurement remain unknown.

Third record: the measure needs no equipment change

The next fictional annual report updates the implementation rate and says the selected action was an operating-schedule change.

Decision: close the equipment-change hypothesis for this item and keep the earlier record as history. C can stay an industry-matched account if the checks still hold. No savings amount, performance result or supply opportunity is established at any point in this example.

07 / decide which research brief you need

Decide which research brief you need

Choose account research when the question is which businesses perform a relevant activity in your territory. A new project is not needed. Specify the operating role and exclusions rather than requiring every account to have an energy announcement. LIEN membership can be one evidence field.

Add project-signal research when a defined change is essential. Agree what qualifies, for example a published Action Plan item or a site-specific proposed change to a fluid system, and which unknowns are acceptable.

Hold an equipment-change claim when the source shows only an assessment, a broad target or an unresolved recommendation. Research fit is not buying readiness, and a project-signal label is not a probability of sale. To weigh a dated signal against fit, use the account prioritisation scoring table.

08 / before handing an efficiency signal to sales

Before handing an efficiency signal to sales

  • ☐ Does the record identify the company, site and relevant system?
  • ☐ Are "planned", "audited", "in the Action Plan", "approved" and "implemented" kept distinct?
  • ☐ Is LIEN membership recorded as fit context with the date checked, not as a project?
  • ☐ Does the signal rule match the evidence, not a product keyword?
  • ☐ Are company fit and project status separate fields?
  • ☐ Are operating changes separated from physical equipment changes?
  • ☐ Are savings described only within their evidenced scope, with nothing transferred or invented?
  • ☐ Does the next check answer a specific missing question, without false urgency?
09 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

Which article of the recast EED covers energy audits?

Article 11 of Directive (EU) 2023/1791 covers energy management systems and energy audits for enterprises.[1] It replaced Article 8 of Directive 2012/27/EU.[2]

Does an energy audit mean a company will buy new equipment?

No. An audit identifies and quantifies saving opportunities. The Action Plan lists measures to implement where technically or economically feasible.[1] Many measures are operating or control changes. Treat any equipment change as unknown until a source states it.

Are energy audit findings public?

The Directive does not require the audit report itself to be published. Article 11(2) requires the Action Plan and the implementation rate to be published in the annual report, subject to confidentiality rules.[1] Check whether a given company's report includes them.

Is a LIEN member a qualified lead?

No. Membership shows a company is a large energy user engaged with SEAI's network.[4] It says nothing about projects, budgets or suppliers.
