What is industrial account research? A definition for industrial suppliers

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 28 October 2026

Industrial account research is the work of finding the companies and sites that genuinely fit an industrial supplier's brief, and recording the public evidence for why each one belongs. Suppliers of pumps, valves and process equipment, and the EPC firms they work with, use it to decide where sales effort goes. Every record carries its reasons, dates and open questions.

Demand Range, which publishes this page, provides industrial account research, opportunity intelligence and B2B buyer intelligence for industrial suppliers in Ireland, the UK and the EU. This page explains the term as we use it, how it differs from related services, how deep to go and what a good account record looks like.

01 / the definition in four parts

The definition in four parts

Industrial account research has four parts. Leave one out and you have something else.

  1. A written brief. The industries, countries, company criteria and exclusions are agreed before any searching starts. The brief is what the research is tested against.
  2. A research unit. Each record is either a company or a named facility, and the two aren't mixed. A group with three plants is not three companies.
  3. Source-supported fit. Each criterion that matters, such as activity, process, size or location, is backed by a public source reference with a date.
  4. Stated uncertainty. Each record says what remains unknown. A licence shows that a process runs on a site. It doesn't show which equipment is installed or who buys it.

Our research method sets out the same steps in full, including entity resolution and review before release.

03 / how it relates to account research in abm

How it relates to account research in ABM

In account-based marketing, "account research" usually means a briefing on one known target account: its priorities, recent news, triggers and the people involved, written so that sales and marketing can tailor their approach.

Industrial account research comes a step earlier and works differently:

  • It decides which accounts belong on the list, rather than briefing on accounts already chosen.
  • It works at site level as often as company level, because an industrial supplier sells into a plant, a terminal or a treatment works, not a head office.
  • It leans on regulatory records, such as licences, hazardous-site lists and tenders, because industrial sites leave a public trail that most other businesses don't.

The two fit together. Once an industrial account list is agreed, the top accounts can get an ABM-style briefing.

04 / how deep to go three levels

How deep to go: three levels

Not every account needs the same effort. We work at three levels, and a brief says which applies to which accounts.

Level Question answered Typical evidence What it doesn't settle
1. Screen Is this company or site in scope at all? Company register status and activity code, website, location Whether the activity code is accurate
2. Verify fit Does it meet each criterion in the brief? A licence, register entry or official list that names the activity at the site, with dates Equipment installed, buying roles
3. Signal check Is anything changing at the site? A tender, planning application, permit review or published investment Whether an order will follow, or when

Most accounts stop at level 2. Level 3 is where opportunity-signal records come from, and it costs the most time per account, so it's worth agreeing in advance which accounts get it.

05 / the public evidence it draws on

The public evidence it draws on

Industrial account research relies on records that public bodies publish for their own purposes. Each answers a narrow question, and reading them correctly is most of the skill.

  • Public tenders. Irish contract notices on eTenders[1] and EU notices on Tenders Electronic Daily (TED)[2] show what a buyer procured and when. See our guides to eTenders and TED.
  • Environmental licences and emissions reporting. The Irish EPA's licensing records[3] and the EU's Industrial Emissions Portal[4] place regulated activities at named facilities. See Irish environmental register evidence, including its PRTR returns section.
  • Company registers. The Companies Registration Office in Ireland[5] and Companies House in the UK[6] confirm legal entities, status and registered activity codes, which helps keep group companies apart.
  • Investment and energy signals. Utility investment plans and energy-efficiency programmes can point to sites with upgrade work under way. See water investment research and energy audit signals.

None of these sources lists buyers, budgets or installed equipment. The research joins them up and says plainly what each one shows.

07 / roles to map not people to list

Roles to map, not people to list

Industrial purchases are rarely made by one person. Account research doesn't have to name contacts to be useful here; it can record which roles matter at the account, and where the public record shows who holds them.

  • Operator or plant owner. The legal entity named on the licence or register, which pays for the equipment.
  • Specifier. Often the plant's own engineering team, sometimes a design consultancy.
  • EPC or EPCM contractor. On a capital project, the contractor may select and buy the equipment. Tender award notices sometimes name them. See our EPC and EPCM supplier research guide.
  • Maintenance and MRO. For spares and service, the buying decision may sit with site maintenance or an outsourced maintenance contractor.

Recording which of these the evidence points to, and which is unknown, tells the sales team where to start.

08 / common mistakes

Common mistakes

  • Treating an activity code as proof. Register codes are chosen by the company and can be out of date. Use them to screen, not to verify.
  • Counting a group as one account, or one site as many. Decide the research unit in the brief and stick to it.
  • Reading a signal as an order. A tender award or planning permission is dated evidence of change, not proof that the supplier's product is needed.
  • Dropping the rejected accounts. A recorded rejection with its reason saves the next researcher repeating the work.
  • Leaving out the dates. A licence or list entry describes the position on a date; without it, nobody can tell when to recheck.
09 / a quick test for any account list

A quick test for any account list

  • ☐ Can you see why each account was included, in one or two sentences?
  • ☐ Does each reason have a source and a date?
  • ☐ Are sites and companies kept apart?
  • ☐ Does each record say what isn't known?
  • ☐ Were any accounts rejected, and is the reason recorded?

If the answer to most of these is no, the list may be useful for volume outreach, but it isn't account research. Industrial account prioritisation covers what to do with a list once it passes.

10 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

What is industrial account research?

It is research that finds the companies and sites fitting an industrial supplier's written brief, and records the dated public evidence for each one, together with what remains unknown.

Is account research the same as lead generation?

No. Lead generation usually aims at contacts or meetings. Account research decides which organisations are worth that effort, and why. The two can work together.

Does industrial account research include contact details?

Not necessarily. At Demand Range, named contacts, email addresses and direct dials are not included by default; the record is about the company or site.

Which sources does it use?

Public registers and notices: tenders, environmental licences and emissions reports, company registers and published investment programmes. The sources depend on the country and the brief.

How is it different from ABM account research?

ABM account research usually briefs a team on an account that has already been chosen. Industrial account research decides which companies and sites belong on the list in the first place, using dated public evidence, often at site level.

Who is it for?

Suppliers of industrial equipment and services, such as pumps, valves, flow control, process equipment, instrumentation and maintenance, and the EPC and EPCM firms that buy for projects.
