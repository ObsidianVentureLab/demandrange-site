Business electricity prices: Ireland compared with the EU

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 27 November 2026

In 2025, Irish businesses using 2,000 to 19,999 MWh a year paid €0.2266/kWh for electricity, excluding recoverable taxes, against €0.1633/kWh for the EU-27 — 38.8% more, by our count. The gap is more than fully accounted for by the energy and supply component: €0.1663/kWh in Ireland against €0.1021/kWh for the EU-27. That component was the highest in the EU-27 in every consumption band, by our count. The figures come from Eurostat's annual electricity price components for non-household consumers, table nrg_pc_205_c.[1][2]

Demand Range researches industrial accounts in Ireland, the UK and the EU. This page owns the price split — energy and supply, network costs, and taxes and levies — for business electricity and gas in Ireland and the other EU countries. It does not cover how much industry uses. For consumption, see industrial energy use by EU country.

01 / key facts

Key facts

Eurostat nrg_pc_205_c and nrg_pc_203_c, 2025, euro per kWh; Commission for Regulation of Utilities network charges; checked 24 November 2026. DESNZ international non-domestic prices, 2025, pence per kWh; checked 27 November 2026.

  • In band ID (2,000 to 19,999 MWh a year), Ireland's energy and supply component was €0.1663/kWh against €0.1021 for the EU-27, and network costs were €0.0530 against €0.0336. The Irish price excluding recoverable taxes was €0.2266/kWh, 38.8% above the EU-27 figure of €0.1633, and 2nd of 27 after Cyprus (€0.2326), by our count.[2]
  • That €0.0633/kWh premium is more than fully explained by energy and supply (+€0.0642). Network costs added €0.0194. Irish taxes and levies excluding recoverable taxes were €0.0203/kWh lower than the EU-27 average, by our count.[2]
  • Ireland had the highest energy and supply component in the EU-27 in every consumption band in 2025 (bands IA to IG, and the all-bands total), by our count of countries with data.[2]
  • Taxes and levies excluding recoverable taxes were 3.2% of the Irish all-bands price, against 16.6% for the EU-27, by our count. Energy and supply was 72.1% of the Irish price against 60.3% for the EU-27.[2]
  • Eurostat's Irish network-cost component (all bands) rose from €0.0357/kWh in 2024 to €0.0586 in 2025, 64.1%, by our count, while the EU-27 figure rose 6.6%. Most of calendar 2025 fell in the Commission for Regulation of Utilities (CRU) 2024/25 tariff year (1 October 2024 to 30 September 2025), for which the CRU estimated a combined average unit price of 7.31c/kWh, about 16% higher than the year before. For 2025/26, from 1 October 2025, it estimated 7.73c/kWh, about 6% higher, and approved 2026 network revenue of €2.72 billion, 7.5% more than 2025. The Eurostat jump is much larger than any of those approved increases, so it should be read with care.[2][3][4][8]
  • For business gas in band I3 (10,000 to 99,999 GJ a year), Irish energy and supply was €0.0413/kWh against €0.0446 for the EU-27 (20th of 25 countries with data). The network component was €0.0173 against €0.0088, about twice the EU-27 figure and 2nd of 25, by our count.[5]
  • In the official DESNZ 2025 annual comparison, medium users (2,000 to 19,999 MWh a year) in the UK paid 25.85 p/kWh for electricity including taxes other than recoverable ones, against 19.45 p/kWh in Ireland. That was the highest of the 28 countries DESNZ compares, 96.27% above the EU-27-plus-UK median of 13.17 p/kWh. The UK figure was 32.9% above Ireland, by our count. These are DESNZ totals in pence per kWh, not Eurostat component prices.[6]

How the prices are built. Energy and supply, network costs, and taxes, fees, levies and charges are official Eurostat cells. Prices and tax lines described as "excluding recoverable taxes" are, by our count, energy and supply plus network costs plus taxes, fees, levies and charges, minus Eurostat's recoverable-tax component. That all-in figure is not the same as Eurostat's half-yearly total-price series, and the two should not be mixed in one comparison. Rankings are by our count of EU-27 countries with a figure in that band. None of the Irish electricity cells used on this page carries a status flag. Eurostat last updated the electricity components dataset on 16 October 2026 at 10:00 Irish time (IST, UTC+1) and the gas dataset on 6 October 2026 at 10:00 Irish time (IST, UTC+1).[1][2][5]

03 / energy network and levies where the irish gap comes from

Energy, network and levies: where the Irish gap comes from

In band ID, the Irish price excluding recoverable taxes was €0.0633/kWh above the EU-27 in 2025, by our count. Energy and supply was €0.0642/kWh higher, so that one line more than accounts for the gap. Network costs were €0.0194/kWh higher. Taxes and levies excluding recoverable taxes were €0.0203/kWh lower than the EU-27 average, which more than offsets the network premium.[2]

The official taxes, fees, levies and charges component (which includes the recoverable-tax element) ranked 22nd of 27 in Ireland in the all-bands total and in bands IC, ID and IE, by our count. In band IG it ranked 16th of the 24 countries with that cell. Across all bands, taxes and levies excluding recoverable taxes were 3.2% of the Irish price, against 16.6% for the EU-27, by our count. Energy and supply was 72.1% of the Irish all-bands price, against 60.3% for the EU-27.[2]

For suppliers of energy efficiency, on-site generation, power purchase agreements or demand response, that split matters. A pitch built on cutting taxes and levies fits the EU-27 average better than it fits Ireland. The Irish cost problem in 2025 sat in the energy and supply line, with a high and rising network line beside it.

The network line needs a caveat. Eurostat's Irish non-household network-cost component rose sharply in 2025, while energy and supply fell:

On 26 August 2025 the CRU approved the annual electricity network charges for the 2025/26 tariff year, effective from 1 October 2025. Its news release states: "In 2026, the approved revenue for distribution is €1,336.80 m and for transmission is €1,389.79 m, €2.72 billion in total. Together, this represents a €190.4 m (7.5%) increase on the 2025 figure of €2.53 billion." The tariff period in the CRU information papers is 1 October 2025 to 30 September 2026. The combined transmission and distribution average unit price for 2025/26 is "estimated to be 7.73c/kWh, which is an increase of circa 6% relative to the current AUP", with distribution expected at 5.13c/kWh (about 10% higher) and transmission estimated at 4.03c/kWh (about 3% higher).[3][4]

For the 2024/25 tariff year (1 October 2024 to 30 September 2025), which covers most of calendar 2025, the CRU estimated the combined transmission and distribution average unit price at 7.31c/kWh, "an increase of circa 16% relative to the current AUP".[8]

Eurostat's 2025 figures are annual, for calendar 2025, so about nine months of them fall in the 2024/25 tariff year and three months in 2025/26. Neither the rise of about 16% for 2024/25 nor the 6–7.5% rises for 2025/26 can, on its own, account for Eurostat's Irish network-cost component rising 64.1% across all bands, or 245.5% in band IG, by our count. Energy and supply fell in the same year. Our inference, not a Eurostat statement, is that some of the movement may reflect a change in how the two lines are split in the returns that feed Eurostat. This page does not treat the 2025 network jump as a like-for-like tariff change, and it does not quote the band IG movement as a tripling of charges. The CRU average unit price and Eurostat's network-cost component are different series and should not be subtracted from one another.[2][3][4][8]

In 2025 Ireland ranked 1st in the EU-27 for the network-cost component in bands IC and IE, 2nd in band ID (after Germany) and in the all-bands total (after Czechia), and 3rd of 21 in band IG, by our count.[2]

Ireland had the highest energy and supply component in every band, by our count: 1st of 27 in bands IA to IE and in the all-bands total, 1st of 25 in band IF, and 1st of 21 in band IG. On the price excluding recoverable taxes, Ireland ranked 1st in bands IB, IC and IG, 2nd in bands ID, IE, IF and the all-bands total (behind Cyprus in each case), and 5th in band IA, by our count. The Irish premium is largest, in percentage terms, in the highest consumption bands: +49.7% in band IE and +55.2% in band IG, by our count. In band IA it is only 3.9%. In that smallest band, Irish network costs (€0.0843/kWh) were below the EU-27 figure (€0.1047/kWh); energy and supply was still the highest in the EU-27.[2]

05 / business gas prices

Business gas prices

The same component split is available for non-household gas in Eurostat table nrg_pc_203_c, also in euro per kWh. The pattern is different from electricity. In 2025 the Irish energy and supply line was at or below the EU-27 average in the mid-to-large bands, while the network line was about twice the EU-27 figure.[5]

In band I3 (10,000 to 99,999 GJ a year; official label "Consumption from 10 000 GJ to 99 999 GJ - band I3"):

  • Energy and supply was €0.0413/kWh in Ireland against €0.0446 for the EU-27, 20th of 25 countries with data, by our count.
  • Network costs were €0.0173/kWh against €0.0088, about 2.0 times the EU-27 figure and 2nd of 25, after Finland, by our count.
  • The Irish price excluding recoverable taxes was €0.0683/kWh against €0.0638, 7.1% higher, by our count.[5]

Across all gas bands, Irish network costs were €0.0162/kWh against €0.0080 for the EU-27, 2.0 times the EU-27 figure and the highest of the 25 countries with data, by our count. In band I4 (100,000 to 999,999 GJ), the Irish price excluding recoverable taxes was €0.0491/kWh against €0.0519 for the EU-27, 5.4% lower, by our count, because energy and supply and taxes and levies were both lower. Ireland's band I5 cells (1,000,000 to 3,999,999 GJ) are confidential, and this page does not quote them. The gas dataset has no Cyprus or Malta rows, so gas ranks are out of 25 EU-27 countries, or fewer where a band is incomplete.[5]

Eurostat last updated the gas components dataset on 6 October 2026 at 10:00 Irish time (IST, UTC+1). None of the Irish gas cells quoted above carries a status flag.[5]

For gas-using sites, the outlier is the network element, which matters for capacity and connection sizing. The commodity line was not the part of the Irish gas bill that sat above the EU-27 in these bands.

06 / the uk where the official figures are

The UK: where the official figures are

In 2025, medium non-domestic electricity users in the UK — those using 2,000 to 19,999 MWh a year, the same range as Eurostat band ID — paid 25.85 p/kWh including taxes other than recoverable ones, against 19.45 p/kWh in Ireland. That was the highest of the 28 countries the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ) compares, 96.27% above the EU-27-plus-UK median of 13.17 p/kWh. The UK figure was 32.9% above Ireland, by our count.[6]

These figures are official annual cells from the DESNZ data set International non-domestic energy prices, in pence per kWh, converted to sterling using the exchange rate at the time of publication for each period. DESNZ derives the comparisons from Eurostat statistics; its workbooks note that UK data no longer form part of the Eurostat dataset but are collected using the same methodology. The official comparison is in pence, and this page does not convert them to euro. The annual figure is the unweighted average of the two half-year periods (January–June and July–December). The electricity workbook's latest half-year is January–June 2026. The gas workbook cover still reads Semester 2 2025 (July–December 2025). The DESNZ landing page was last updated on 26 November 2026. Both the electricity and gas workbooks were published on 26 November 2026.[6]

Eurostat's nrg_pc_205_c and nrg_pc_203_c datasets used here have no United Kingdom cells for 2024 or 2025, so this page does not place a UK row in the EU country tables and does not rank the UK against EU countries on those components.[2][5] DESNZ figures are in pence per kWh and have no energy, network and levies split, so they sit in their own table and are not ranked against the Eurostat component figures above. This page does not use older Eurostat UK cells as a substitute for the current DESNZ tables. The DESNZ comparator is the EU-27-plus-UK median, not Eurostat's EU-27 aggregate, and DESNZ ranks from lowest to highest (1 is the cheapest). The earlier GOV.UK path /government/statistical-data-sets/international-industrial-energy-prices now redirects to the current data set.[6]

For non-domestic gas in 2025, on the same including-taxes-other-than-recoverable-ones basis, medium users (2,778–27,777 MWh a year, about Eurostat band I3) paid 4.67 p/kWh in the UK against 5.87 p/kWh in Ireland, and the EU-27-plus-UK median was 5.06 p/kWh. Large users (27,778–277,777 MWh, about band I4) paid 4.12 p/kWh in the UK against 4.21 p/kWh in Ireland. Unlike electricity, the official UK gas figures in these bands sit below Ireland.[6]

These national price tables do not name any company, but they tell you which part of the bill is high, and in which consumption band. That is a starting point for sizing demand for efficiency, on-site generation and connection work:

  1. Start from the component, not the headline average. In Ireland the 2025 electricity premium was in energy and supply, with network costs also high. Taxes and levies were not the driver.
  2. Match the band to the account. The Irish premium is much larger in bands IE and IG than in band IA. Use the band that fits the site's annual use, not the all-bands total alone.
  3. Pair prices with energy use. This page is the price split; industrial energy use by EU country is the consumption split. Together they separate a high unit price from a high volume of use.
  4. Look for dated Irish triggers. A national average does not time outreach. For energy-audit and large-user signals, see energy-audit signals for industrial suppliers. For large electricity users in the data-centre sector, see data centre construction research for suppliers, and read the price components on this page next to that demand.

07 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

Why is business electricity more expensive in Ireland than the EU average?
Mainly the energy and supply component. In 2025, in the 2,000 to 19,999 MWh band, Eurostat nrg_pc_205_c puts it at €0.1663/kWh in Ireland against €0.1021 for the EU-27, which more than covers the gap. The Irish price excluding recoverable taxes was €0.2266/kWh, 38.8% above the EU-27's €0.1633, by our count. Irish taxes and levies excluding recoverable taxes were lower.[2]
Are taxes and levies why Irish industrial electricity costs more?
No. In 2025, taxes and levies excluding recoverable taxes were 3.2% of the Irish all-bands price, against 16.6% for the EU-27, by our count. Ireland's taxes, fees, levies and charges component ranked 22nd of 27. The premium sits in energy and supply, the highest in the EU in every band, and in network costs.[2]
Did Irish network charges rise sharply in 2025?
On Eurostat's figures, yes: the network-cost component for Irish non-household electricity rose from €0.0357 to €0.0586/kWh in 2025 (all bands), 64.1%, by our count. The CRU's tariff decisions point to much smaller rises, about 16% from October 2024 and about 6% from October 2025, so the Eurostat jump should be read with care.[2][4][8]
How do Irish business gas prices compare with the EU average?
In 2025, in the 10,000 to 99,999 GJ band, Irish energy and supply was €0.0413/kWh, 20th of 25 EU countries with data, against €0.0446 for the EU-27. The network component was €0.0173 against €0.0088, about twice the EU-27 figure and 2nd of 25, by our count. The Irish price excluding recoverable taxes was 7.1% above the EU-27, by our count.[5]
Does this page include UK business electricity prices?
Yes, from DESNZ rather than Eurostat. In 2025, medium users (2,000 to 19,999 MWh) in the UK paid 25.85p/kWh including taxes other than recoverable ones, against 19.45p in Ireland, the highest of the 28 countries DESNZ compares. DESNZ gives no energy, network and levies split, so the UK is not ranked in the Eurostat tables.[6]
