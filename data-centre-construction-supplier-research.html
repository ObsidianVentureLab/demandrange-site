Research guide · Source evidence and limits

Data centre construction suppliers Ireland

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 10 November 2026

Data centres used 23% of Ireland's metered electricity in 2025, and a new site typically needs cooling, pumps, valves, water treatment, fire protection and generation. Official statistics show scale. Planning files and grid rules show whether a named project exists. A rumour or unofficial list is not a project.

Demand Range does evidence-backed industrial account research for Ireland, the UK and the EU. This page is a sector route-map for Irish data-centre construction. It is not an energy audit, a grid-connection application, legal advice or a list of sites to call.

01 / official electricity figures show scale not a building programme

Official electricity figures show scale, not a building programme

The Central Statistics Office (CSO) published Data Centres Metered Electricity Consumption 2025 on 7 July 2026. The statistician's comment is dated 7 July 2026; if a header on the release shows "07 July 2025", that is not the publication year.[1]

In 2025, data centres used 7,663 GWh, 23% of all metered electricity consumption in Ireland, up from 6,973 GWh and 22% in 2024 and from 5% in 2015. That 2025 total was 10% higher than 2024, while consumption by all other users rose by 2%. Total metered electricity consumption rose by 34% between 2015 and 2025.[1]

Two other official figures are easy to mix up with that 23% share, and should be kept distinct:

  • Large energy users, which include significant data centres, accounted for the largest overall share of total consumption at 33% in 2025. Their annual usage rose 9% since 2024, to 10,812 GWh.[1]
  • Quarterly data-centre use reached 1,991 GWh in the fourth quarter of 2025, against 291 GWh in the first quarter of 2015, a rise of 584%.[1]

The CSO's 2023 release put the data-centre share at 21% for 2023.[10] A figure of about 21% therefore describes 2023; the official 2025 share is 23%.[1]

These figures do not tell you where the next hall will be built or what equipment it will buy. The CSO does not publish a site list. It identifies data centres in the metered data by searching for known operators, using industry reports and internet searches, and examining high-consumption customers.[1] Use the latest release in the CSO series, and quote its publication date with any figure.[2]

Electricity use is an energy-demand signal. For how to read energy-audit and large-user energy evidence without inventing a construction job, see energy-audit signals for industrial suppliers.

03 / what the planning data can and cannot show

What the planning data can, and cannot, show

The national planning applications dataset on data.gov.ie is the starting register.[3] Its catalogue date is not the currency of the live map service. When we queried the Planning Application Points service on 10 November 2026, its last edit time was 12:26 Irish time (UTC+0) that day, and the latest received date in the service was 6 November 2026.[4]

By our count, the service held 511,965 application points that day. Of those, 99 had a development description containing "data centre" or "data center".[4] Within those 99 descriptions, again by our count and using case-insensitive whole-word matches:

  • 57 mentioned a generator or generators
  • 53 mentioned a tank or tanks
  • 48 mentioned a substation
  • 37 mentioned the whole word water
  • 35 mentioned a pump or pumps
  • 31 mentioned a sprinkler or sprinklers
  • 10 mentioned a chiller or chillers

These are text matches in the development description, not a census of data-centre equipment. A description can mention water for a drainage connection, or omit a chiller that appears only in the drawings. "Wastewater" or "firewater" is not counted in the whole-word "water" figure. By our count, the 99 records were spread across 13 planning authorities, and wording is not uniform. The latest received date among those 99, again by our count, was 27 May 2026, which is earlier than the latest received date in the full dataset, so this text filter is a starting list, not a live feed of every recent application.[4]

Separately, by our count, 26 of the 99 also mentioned a data hall, and 11 further records mentioned a data hall without mentioning a data centre or data center. Do not treat those 11 as data-centre projects until you have read the file.[4]

Open each candidate on the authority's portal. Record the application number, the decision and the source date. A granted application is a stronger signal than a lodged one. A lodged application is still only an application. It is not a construction start, a purchase or a supplier appointment.

04 / grid rules that create separate equipment packages

Grid rules that create separate equipment packages

On 12 December 2025 the CRU published its decision on the Large Energy Users connection policy (CRU/2025236). The policy "will apply exclusively to all data centres seeking to connect to the electricity network." The CRU's decision page says new data centres connecting under the policy "will be required to provide new renewable and dispatchable electricity generation."[6][7]

The decision sets a de-minimis level of 1 MVA of maximum import capacity (MIC). Below that level, no other requirements arise from this policy, though the system operators still assess whether a connection can be accommodated.[7]

From 1 MVA up to, but not including, 10 MVA, a data centre must provide an autoproducer unit that meets 100% of the site's MIC on a de-rated basis and participates in the Single Electricity Market.[7]

At 10 MVA or more, a data centre must provide dispatchable on-site or proximate generation and/or storage to a level that at least covers its MIC, subject to derating. That generation must be separately connected and metered, and must participate in the wholesale electricity market. The site cannot be operational, or ramp up to its full MIC, without delivery of the associated generation.[7]

Data centres at or above the 1 MVA de-minimis level must meet at least 80% of annual demand with additional renewable electricity generated in the Republic of Ireland, with a six-year glide path from the date of the demand site's energisation.[7]

EirGrid's Version 3 process, dated 20 May 2026, applies that framework to new transmission connection applications of 1 MVA or more received on or after 12 December 2025. A data-centre application package comprises at least three linked but separate applications: the data centre, the nominated generation, and the nominated renewables. The generation developer may be a different legal entity from the data-centre customer.[8]

For suppliers, that split matters. Gas engines, batteries, fuel systems, cooling water and fire protection for an energy centre may be bought by a different company from the one building the data halls. Do not assume a single buyer, and do not treat a connection policy as evidence that a particular site has applied. Our guide to EPC and EPCM equipment packages covers how to trace who holds each package once a project is in public view.

06 / a method from a planning record to a researched account

A method: from a planning record to a researched account

  1. Pull candidates from the public register. Filter the national dataset for "data centre", "data center" and "data hall", then add generation, energy-centre and substation applications that the same file, or a linked file, ties to the same site. Do not add a site because of a rumour.
  2. Open the file. Use the dataset's link to the authority's portal. Read the description, drawings and reports for plant, tanks, generators and water.
  3. Record the stage. Note the received date, decision, any further-information request and any appeal. Keep "lodged", "further information", "granted", "refused" and "under appeal" distinct.
  4. Find the linked projects. Look for separate applications for generation, storage, grid or water connections on or near the site. Under the CRU and EirGrid rules, those may have a different applicant.
  5. Identify the buyer route as an open question. The package may sit with the developer, a main contractor, a mechanical and electrical contractor or an EPC firm. Record it as unknown until a public source says otherwise.
  6. Write the account record. Keep the applicant, application number, packages evidenced, source dates and open questions together. Our sample opportunity shows the format.

Avoid copying applicant names that belong to private individuals into a contact list. Record the company, where there is one, and the application reference instead.

07 / the uk in brief

The UK in brief

In the UK, the government designated data centres as Critical National Infrastructure on 12 September 2024.[9] Planning applications are held by each local planning authority, so the same method applies, authority by authority: cite only a public application reference, and do not treat an unofficial project list as evidence. Demand Range covers Ireland, the UK and the EU; this page focuses on Irish public sources.

08 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

How much electricity do Irish data centres use?
According to the Central Statistics Office, data centres used 7,663 GWh in 2025, which was 23% of all metered electricity consumption, up from 6,973 GWh and 22% in 2024.[1] Large energy users, which include significant data centres, accounted for 33% and 10,812 GWh.[1] These are national totals, not a list of sites or a construction programme.
Can I get a list of data-centre sites from the CSO?
No. The CSO publishes totals only. It identifies data centres in the metered-electricity data by searching for known operators, using industry reports and internet searches, and examining high-consumption customers.[1] The figures describe the sector's electricity use. They do not name sites, applicants or equipment packages.
How do I find data-centre planning applications in Ireland?
Filter the national planning applications dataset for "data centre", "data center" and "data hall" in the development description, then open each file on the planning authority's portal.[3][4] By our count, 99 records matched "data centre" or "data center" on 10 November 2026, and 11 more mentioned only a data hall.[4] Read each file before treating it as a project.
Why do the CRU connection rules matter to equipment suppliers?
New data centres connecting under the policy must provide new renewable and dispatchable electricity generation.[6] Sites of 10 MVA or more must provide separately connected, market-participating generation or storage, and sites from 1 MVA must meet an 80% renewable requirement over a six-year glide path.[7] Those assets can be developed by a different company from the data-centre customer.[8]
What equipment do data-centre planning descriptions mention?
Generators, most often. By our count, of the 99 data-centre descriptions in the national planning dataset on 10 November 2026, 57 mentioned generators, 53 tanks, 48 substations, 35 pumps, 31 sprinklers and 10 chillers.[4] These are text matches, not an equipment census. The drawings and reports in the authority's file show more.
