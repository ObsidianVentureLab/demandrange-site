Hourly manufacturing labour costs by EU country, 2025

By Demand Range research team · Last reviewed 24 November 2026

In 2025, Ireland's hourly labour cost in manufacturing was 42.4 euro, against 35.0 euro in the EU-27, a gap of 7.4 euro per hour by our count.[1][2] Among the 25 EU countries with a published 2025 cell, Ireland ranked 8th by our count. Among all 29 countries Eurostat published in this dataset, including four countries outside the EU, Ireland ranked 10th by our count.[1][2] The figures are hourly labour-cost levels from Eurostat dataset lc_lci_lev, for NACE Rev. 2 manufacturing (Section C), in euro. They measure labour cost for the labour cost index (compensation of employees plus taxes minus subsidies). The latest published year is 2025. The dataset was last updated on 23 April 2026, 22:00 Irish time (UTC+01:00), and was checked on 24 November 2026.[1][2]

A separate quarterly labour cost index, described further down this page, tracks how manufacturing labour costs have moved into 2026. The two series are not interchangeable.[3][4]

01 / key facts

Key facts

Eurostat lc_lci_lev, data updated 23 April 2026, 22:00 Irish time (UTC+01:00); Eurostat lc_lci_r2_q, data updated 19 November 2026, 10:00 Irish time (GMT, UTC+0); checked 24 November 2026.

  • Ireland 42.4 euro per hour in manufacturing in 2025, against 35.0 euro in the EU-27. The gap is 7.4 euro per hour, by our count. Neither cell carries a flag.[1][2]
  • From 2024 to 2025 the Irish hourly figure rose from 40.8 to 42.4 euro, a 3.9% increase by our count. The EU-27 figure rose from 33.8 to 35.0 euro, a 3.6% increase by our count.[1][2]
  • Among the 25 EU countries with a published 2025 cell, Denmark was highest, at 55.0 euro per hour, and Bulgaria was lowest, at 10.2 euro. Ireland was 8th, by our count. Austria's 2025 figure, 51.3 euro, is provisional (P).[1][2]
  • Belgium's 2025 hourly figure is not published in this dataset; its latest published figure is 51.5 euro per hour in 2024. The Netherlands' 2025 cell is confidential (C); its latest published figure is 47.3 euro per hour in 2024.[1][2]
  • The quarterly labour cost index for manufacturing (index, 2020=100, calendar adjusted, not seasonally adjusted) stood at 131.2 in the EU-27 in 2026-Q2, up 3.0% year on year from 127.4 in 2025-Q2, by our count. Ireland stood at 137.6 (P), up 4.6% year on year from 131.6, by our count.[3][4]
  • Among EU countries with a published 2026-Q2 and 2025-Q2 index cell, Bulgaria had the largest year-on-year rise, at 11.6% (P on both quarters), and Austria the smallest, at 1.7% (P on 2026-Q2), by our count.[3][4]

03 / ireland against the eu hourly levels

Ireland against the EU: hourly levels

Ireland's hourly labour-cost level in manufacturing has stayed above the EU-27 figure in every published year from 2020 to 2025. The 2025 gap of 7.4 euro per hour, by our count, is the difference between the two published cells for that year, 42.4 and 35.0. In 2022 the figures were 36.5 euro in Ireland and 30.4 euro in the EU-27; in 2023 they were 38.5 and 32.1.[1][2]

04 / the labour cost index into 2026

The labour cost index into 2026

The labour cost index is a different series from the hourly levels above. It does not state a euro-per-hour amount. It measures the change in the same labour-cost component (compensation of employees plus taxes minus subsidies) in manufacturing, as an index with 2020=100. The series used here is calendar adjusted, not seasonally adjusted. Year-on-year changes are the better cost-pressure reading on this series; quarter-on-quarter moves can be seasonal, so they are not used as a headline here.[3][4]

In 2026-Q2 the EU-27 index stood at 131.2, up 3.0% from 127.4 in 2025-Q2, by our count. Ireland stood at 137.6 (P), up 4.6% from 131.6, by our count. Ireland's 2026-Q2 cell is provisional; the 2025-Q2 Irish cell and both EU-27 cells carry no flag. The latest published quarter for this manufacturing, calendar-adjusted, 2020=100 series is 2026-Q2. The dataset was last updated on 19 November 2026, 10:00 Irish time (GMT, UTC+0), and was checked on 24 November 2026.[3][4]

Among the 25 EU countries with both a 2026-Q2 and a 2025-Q2 cell, Bulgaria had the largest year-on-year rise, at 11.6% (P on both quarters), and Austria the smallest, at 1.7% (P on 2026-Q2), by our count. Belgium has no 2026-Q2 cell in this dataset (its latest is 120.6 (P) in 2025-Q2). The Netherlands' 2026-Q2 cell is confidential (C).[3][4]

The United Kingdom is not in this dataset.

05 / using labour cost figures as a cost pressure signal

Using labour-cost figures as a cost-pressure signal

Use the hourly levels to compare what an hour of manufacturing labour cost in 2025, country by country. Use the index to see whether those costs were still rising or easing into 2026. Keep the two series in separate comparisons: an hourly figure and an index point are not the same measure.[1][3]

A high hourly level, as in Denmark or Ireland, is not the same thing as a fast recent rise. Some EU countries with much lower 2025 hourly levels had larger year-on-year index increases to 2026-Q2, by our count. Treat the figures as a territory-screening signal, not as a wage offer, a contract price or a plant-level labour cost.[1][3][4]

Three further caveats. First, some 2025 level cells and some 2026-Q2 index cells are provisional (P) or confidential (C), and two EU members have no 2025 hourly level in this dataset. Second, the counts describe NACE Rev. 2 manufacturing and the published labour-cost component; they may not match a local plant's own wage bill. Third, four countries outside the EU appear in the levels dataset; they are labelled as such and are not in the EU rankings. Demand Range uses official labour-cost figures when choosing countries and sectors, then builds a named account list from public sources, with the evidence for each row. Request a written scope if you want that work done on a territory.

06 / frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

What does an hour of manufacturing labour cost in the EU?
Eurostat's hourly labour-cost level for EU-27 manufacturing was 35.0 euro in 2025, up from 33.8 euro in 2024, a 3.6% rise by our count. The figure covers NACE Rev. 2 manufacturing and measures labour cost for the labour cost index (compensation of employees plus taxes minus subsidies). It is a level in euro per hour, not the quarterly index.[1][2]
What does an hour of manufacturing labour cost in Ireland?
Eurostat's hourly labour-cost level for Irish manufacturing was 42.4 euro in 2025, up from 40.8 euro in 2024, a 3.9% rise by our count. That is 7.4 euro per hour above the EU-27 figure, 8th of the 25 EU countries with a published 2025 cell, and 10th of 29 published countries in this dataset, by our count.[1][2]
Which EU country has the highest manufacturing labour cost per hour?
Denmark, at 55.0 euro per hour in 2025, among the 25 EU countries with a published cell, by our count. Austria is next, at 51.3 euro (P), then Germany at 49.5. In the wider published series, Iceland is higher than any EU member, at 61.2 euro (P). Bulgaria is the lowest published EU member, at 10.2 euro.[1][2]
How fast are EU manufacturing labour costs rising?
The EU-27 labour cost index (2020=100, calendar adjusted, not seasonally adjusted) stood at 131.2 in 2026-Q2, up 3.0% year on year, by our count. Ireland stood at 137.6 (P), up 4.6%. Among EU countries with both quarters published, Bulgaria had the largest rise, at 11.6% (P on both quarters), and Austria the smallest, at 1.7% (P on 2026-Q2), by our count.[3][4]
Does this Eurostat table include a UK manufacturing figure?
No. The United Kingdom is not in the 2025 hourly-level dataset or the 2026-Q2 manufacturing labour cost index used on this page. The EU aggregate is EU-27 (from 2020). Four countries outside the EU — Iceland, Norway, Serbia and Türkiye — do appear in the levels dataset and are labelled separately.[1][3]
How should I use labour-cost figures as a cost-pressure signal?
Use the 2025 hourly levels to compare countries in one year, and the quarterly index to track change into 2026. Do not mix a euro-per-hour level with an index point, or treat either as a wage offer or a plant-level cost. Allow for provisional and confidential cells. Demand Range can turn the official figures into a named account list.[1][3]
